how language models think

BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · artificial intelligence — predicting the next word, one step at a time, with attention deciding what to look back at

A large language model does one thing: given some text, it estimates the probability of every possible next piece of text, picks one, and repeats. Everything else (answering, translating, writing code) emerges from doing that extremely well. The key mechanism is attention, which lets each word look back at the words that matter to it.

01The one job

Predict the next word, pick one, repeat

Text is first chopped into tokens, whole words or pieces of words. The model reads all the tokens so far and outputs a score for every token in its vocabulary, often over 100,000 of them. Those scores become probabilities.

Then one token is sampled: drawn at random, weighted by probability. Temperature controls how adventurous the draw is. Low temperature nearly always takes the top choice and can get repetitive; high temperature flattens the odds and gets creative, then incoherent. The chosen token is added to the text and the whole process runs again, one token at a time.

This little demo predicts from simple word-pair statistics of a short text written for this lesson. A real model does the same final step, but its probabilities come from a neural network with billions of parameters that has read a large fraction of the written internet.

The softmax turns scores z into probabilities; dividing by T sharpens (T < 1) or flattens (T > 1) them. Here z is the log of how often each word followed the previous two words (a trigram model, falling back to one word) in a corpus of about a hundred sentences. Entropy is measured in bits: 1 bit is a coin toss.

02Attention

A tiny transformer you can poke

Confidence—
Copied from—

The breakthrough behind today’s models is the transformer (2017). Each position in the text asks a question (a query), every earlier position advertises what it holds (a key), and a match sends information back (a value). Positions attend to the ones that answer their question. Stack dozens of layers, each with many such heads, and the model can follow grammar, references and arguments across thousands of words.

This is a real two-layer attention model running in your browser, small enough to read. In layer 1, every character attends to the one just before it (and, in a second head, the one before that) and stores what it saw. In layer 2, the last character asks: “where earlier did the same pair of characters as me and my neighbour appear?” It attends there and copies the character that came next.

This pattern, the induction head, was found inside trained language models in 2022. It is one way models pick up patterns from the text in front of them, part of how they learn “in context”.

Try thisPress Append prediction a few times and watch it complete the repeated word. Then type your own repeating text.
The math
Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK⊤/√d + mask) V

The two heads compute real softmax attention over vectors, with a causal mask so no position sees the future. Unlike a trained model, the weights here were set by hand: layer 1’s two heads match each position to the one and two places before, and layer 2 matches the current character and its predecessor against those stored characters, with a small bonus for recent positions. Real models learn millions of such weights from data.

03Meaning as geometry

Words as arrows in space

Result lands nearest—
Similarity—

Inside the model, every token becomes a long list of numbers, an embedding, a point in a space with thousands of dimensions. Training arranges these points so that words used in similar ways end up close together.

Directions in that space come to mean something. The step from “man” to “woman” points roughly the same way as the step from “king” to “queen”, so arithmetic on meanings works: king − man + woman ≈ queen. This was a famous finding of word2vec in 2013.

In a modern model the vectors are refined layer by layer as attention mixes in context, so “bank” near “river” ends up somewhere different from “bank” near “money”.

A toy: 22 words with hand-made vectors in eleven dimensions (royal, male, adult, young, animal, city, country, plus one for each of four nations) and a little noise. For each analogy the picture is a flat slice through that space, chosen to contain both arrows, so other words can appear oddly placed. Real embeddings are learned, not designed, and their dimensions rarely line up with neat human labels.

Observations

Milestones

1948

Shannon’s n-grams

Claude Shannon generated English-like text by sampling each word from the words that followed the previous ones, the same trick as Bay 01, and used it to measure the information in language.

2013

word2vec

Tomas Mikolov’s team at Google showed that simple networks learn word vectors where analogies become arithmetic.

2017

“Attention Is All You Need”

Eight Google researchers introduced the transformer, dropping older recurrent designs. It trains in parallel on huge amounts of text and underlies almost every modern language model.

2020

Scaling laws

Researchers found that a model’s prediction error falls smoothly and predictably as model size, data and computing power grow, which drove the race to ever larger models.
