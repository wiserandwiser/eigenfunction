seeing a black hole

ASTROPHYSICS · strong-field gravity, imaging — you can’t photograph darkness, but you can photograph the shadow it casts on light

A black hole gives out no light, yet in 2019 the world saw one. What the picture shows is a shadow: a dark patch, wider than the black hole itself, framed by gas glowing as it swirls in, its light bent into a ring. Taking it needed a telescope as wide as the Earth.

01Ray-traced

What a black hole with a disk of gas looks like

Shadow across—
Brighter side—
What you’re seeing—

Gas falling into a black hole forms a flat, spinning disk that heats to millions of degrees and glows. Near the black hole, gravity bends light so strongly that the image is warped. Light from the far side of the disk curves over the top and bottom of the black hole, so you see the back of the disk even though the black hole is in the way.

The gas orbits at a large fraction of the speed of light, so the side moving towards you is boosted much brighter than the side moving away: Doppler beaming.

At 1.5 times the horizon radius, light can orbit the black hole. Light that grazes this orbit loops round before escaping, making a thin, sharp photon ring. Inside it, lines of sight end on the horizon, so you see a dark shadow about 2.6 times wider than the horizon itself.

Try thisTilt from nearly edge-on to face-on and watch the far side of the disk wrap over the top. Turn on the horizon outline to compare it with the shadow.
The math
bcrit = √27 GM/c² ≈ 2.6 rs,  1 + z = (1 + Ω b sin i sin α) / √(1 − 3GM/rc²)

Each pixel traces a light ray backwards through the Schwarzschild geometry (a non-spinning black hole), integrating d²u/dφ² = 3GM u²/c² − u, with u = 1/r, until it hits the disk, falls in or escapes. The disk runs from the innermost stable orbit (6 GM/c², three horizon radii) outward. Brightness scales as (1 + z)−4 to include Doppler beaming and gravitational redshift; Ω is the gas’s orbital rate. This follows Jean-Pierre Luminet’s 1979 method.

02An Earth-sized telescope

Sharpen the picture by spreading out the dishes

Sharpest detail—
Smallest thing it could see on the Moon—
Verdict—

The black hole in the galaxy M87 is 6.5 billion times the Sun’s mass, but 55 million light-years away. Its shadow spans about 40 microarcseconds on the sky, as small as a doughnut on the Moon looks from Earth.

A telescope’s sharpness depends on its width compared with the wavelength it observes. To see the shadow at 1.3 mm radio wavelength would take a dish nearly as wide as the planet. The Event Horizon Telescope fakes one: radio dishes from Hawaii to the South Pole record the same waves at the same moment, timed by atomic clocks, and supercomputers combine the recordings. Earth’s rotation sweeps the dishes around, filling in more of the giant virtual mirror.

The data from April 2017 filled about 5 petabytes, so much that hard drives were flown to the correlators; the South Pole’s had to wait for the end of the Antarctic winter. The picture was released on 10 April 2019.

Try thisGrow the telescope from a single dish to the whole Earth. Find the size where the dark centre first appears.
The math
θ ≈ λ / D,  λ = 1.3 mm,  D = 10,000 km → θ ≈ 27 μas

The image is Bay 01’s model at a 17° tilt (roughly how we view M87’s jet), scaled to M87’s shadow, blurred by a Gaussian of width λ/D. The real EHT reconstructs images from sparse data with careful algorithms and checks them against many simulations; the real M87 ring is thicker because the glowing gas is a hot, puffy flow, not a thin disk.

03Two shadows

A giant far away, a small one close by

Mass · distance—
Shadow on our sky—
Gas circles the inner disk in—

The shadow’s size is set by the black hole’s mass; how big it looks depends on its distance. M87’s giant and our own galaxy’s central black hole, Sagittarius A*, are about 1,600 times apart in mass and 2,000 times apart in distance, so they look about the same size.

Sagittarius A* was much harder. Gas circles the giant in M87 in days, so its image held still over a night of observing. Around Sagittarius A* it circles in minutes, so the target changed while the picture was being taken. Its image took until May 2022 and needed thousands of simulations to understand.

Its mass was already known from stars. Astronomers followed stars orbiting the galaxy’s centre for decades; one, S2, swings round every 16 years at up to almost 3% of the speed of light. That work won the 2020 Nobel Prize.

The math
θshadow = 2√27 GM / (c² D),  TISCO = 2π · 63/2 GM/c³

Shadow sizes for a non-spinning black hole: M87* ≈ 40 μas (the observed ring is about 42 μas), Sagittarius A* ≈ 50 μas (observed about 52). Orbit times are at the innermost stable orbit. Masses and distances: M87* 6.5 × 10⁹ Suns, 55 million light-years; Sagittarius A* 4.0 × 10⁶ Suns, 27,000 light-years; Andromeda’s ~1.4 × 10⁸ Suns, 2.5 million light-years; Cygnus X-1 ~21 Suns, 7,200 light-years.

Observations

The road to the picture

1916

Schwarzschild’s solution

Weeks after Einstein published general relativity, Karl Schwarzschild, serving on the Eastern Front, found the exact solution for a point mass. It contains the horizon, though no one took it seriously as a real object for decades.

1979

Luminet’s drawing

Jean-Pierre Luminet computed the first image of a black hole with a glowing disk on an early computer, then drew the result by hand in ink, dot by dot. It looks remarkably like this lesson’s Bay 01.

2019

M87*

The first image of a black hole’s shadow: an orange ring brighter at the bottom, where gas moves towards us. Polarised light images in 2021 traced its magnetic fields.

2022

Sagittarius A*

The black hole at the heart of the Milky Way, imaged by the same network. Three bright knots on a ring; the knots are probably artefacts of its fast flicker and sparse data.
