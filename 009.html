spin

QUANTUM · spin — a built-in magnetism that, whenever you measure it, comes out as only up or down

Electrons behave like tiny magnets. Ask which way one points and the answer is never “a bit to the left”. It is always exactly up or exactly down, along whatever direction you chose to ask about. Chain those questions together and the answers start to rewrite each other.

011922

A beam that splits in two, never smears

Possible answers—

In 1922 Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach fired silver atoms from a hot oven through a magnet with a specially shaped, uneven field. Each silver atom carries one unpaired electron, and that electron acts like a tiny bar magnet. The uneven field pushes the atom up or down depending on which way its little magnet points.

The atoms leave the oven pointing in random directions. Common sense says you should see a smear: atoms pointing straight up deflected most, sideways ones not at all, everything in between. Instead the atoms landed in exactly two spots, one up and one down, with nothing in the middle.

Gerlach sent Niels Bohr a postcard with the photograph. Legend has it that the faint silver deposit only showed up once it was exposed to sulphur from cheap cigar smoke. The property they had uncovered was later named spin (Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit, 1925).

Try thisRun the classical prediction, then switch to what actually happens. Count how many in-between values ever appear.
The math
Fz = μz ∂B∂z,  Sz = ±ħ2

The force depends on the magnetic moment’s component along the field gradient. Classically μz = μ cos θ takes every value from −μ to +μ, giving a continuous smear. For an electron, the spin component along any axis has only two possible values, ±ħ/2, so the beam splits into exactly two.

02Chaining questions

Ask a new question and the old answer is erased

Theory says—

Each box here is a spin analyser: a Stern–Gerlach magnet that asks one question, “up or down along my axis?”, and sends each atom out of one of two exits. We keep only the up exit and block the other.

Up? Up? Ask the same question twice and you get the same answer every time: 100% up. So far, so sensible.

Up? Sideways? Take atoms known to be up and ask whether they point left or right. The answer is 50/50. An atom that was definitely up has no sideways value at all.

Up? Sideways? Up? Now ask “up?” again. You blocked every down atom at the first box, yet half now come out down. The sideways question wiped out the earlier answer. Spin along different axes can’t be known at the same time, just like position and momentum in Φ 008.

Try thisRun the three experiments in order. Then pick two analysers and slide the second angle slowly from 0° to 180°, watching the up share follow a smooth curve.
The math
P(up along θ | up along 0) = cos²(θ/2)

The probabilities depend on half the angle between the two axes. At 90° that gives cos²(45°) = ½; at 180° it gives 0. Every atom is simulated: an unpolarised source picks a random direction, and each analyser makes a random up/down choice with these odds and resets the spin to its own axis.

03Any angle

The odds follow half the angle

Measurements0

Prepare a spin pointing straight up. Then measure it along an axis tilted by any angle. Drag the axis around the dial.

Each single measurement still says only “along” or “against” the axis. Which one you get is random, but the odds depend smoothly on the tilt: certain at 0°, a coin toss at 90°, impossible at 180°. The curve is cos² of half the angle.

That half-angle hides one of the strangest facts in physics. Rotate an electron by a full 360° and its quantum state flips sign. It takes 720° to get it all the way back. Neutron experiments in 1975 confirmed this directly. Spin isn’t a little ball rotating. It is something with no everyday counterpart.

Try thisSet the axis to 90° and fire 500: about half and half. Then try 60° and 120°, and check the dots against the curve.
The math
|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|↑θ⟩ + sin(θ/2)|↓θ⟩

The up state, written in the basis of an axis tilted by θ. Squaring the amplitudes gives the odds. Under a rotation by angle α, a spin-½ state picks up a factor e−iασ/2, so α = 2π gives −1 and only α = 4π returns +1.

Observations

Spin in your life

Hospitals

MRI scanners

The protons in your body’s water have spin too. In a strong magnet they line up, wobble at a precise rate (about 42.6 million times a second per tesla) and broadcast radio signals that become images of soft tissue.

Your hard drive

Spintronics

Hard-drive read heads use giant magnetoresistance: current flows more easily when electron spins line up with the layers they pass through. Its discovery won the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Precision

The best-tested prediction

The electron’s magnetic strength, its “g-factor”, is just over 2. Theory and experiment agree to about one part in a trillion, the most precise match between prediction and measurement in all of science.

Qubits

Up and down make a natural pair of 0 and 1. A spin in a superposition of both is a qubit, the building block of the quantum computers in Φ 015.
