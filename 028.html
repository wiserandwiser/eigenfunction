BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · materials — metals that lose all electrical resistance, push magnetic fields out, and make the strongest magnets on Earth

Cool certain materials enough and their electrical resistance does not just get small: it vanishes. A current set running in a superconducting loop has been watched for years without fading. They power every MRI scanner and the Large Hadron Collider, and new ones that work at higher temperatures may make fusion power plants small enough to build.

01Zero resistance

Cool it down, and the friction disappears

State—
Resistance—
Cooling needed—

In an ordinary wire, electrons carrying a current keep bumping into the crystal’s vibrating atoms and its flaws, losing energy as heat. That is resistance. Cooling the metal calms the vibrations, but some resistance always remains.

In 1911 Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled mercury with liquid helium and watched its resistance drop to zero at 4.2 K. The explanation took until 1957: below the critical temperature, electrons form loosely bound Cooper pairs, glued by tiny ripples in the crystal. The pairs all fall into one shared quantum state, and a single collision can’t knock one out without disrupting them all. So the whole flow moves as one, with no friction.

In 1986 a new family, the copper-oxide cuprates, broke the rules: YBCO superconducts at 92 K, warm enough for cheap liquid nitrogen. How they work is still not fully understood.

02Pushing fields out

The Meissner effect and magnetic vortices

State—
Field inside—

A superconductor is more than a perfect conductor. In 1933 Walther Meissner and Robert Ochsenfeld found that cooling one below its critical temperature actively pushes magnetic fields out. Currents flowing in its skin create a field that exactly cancels the one outside. This is why a magnet can hover over it.

Raise the field and eventually it wins. In type II superconductors, which include all the useful ones, the field gets in gradually, as thin vortices: whirlpools of current each carrying one quantum of magnetic flux. Between them the material stays superconducting.

If the vortices are pinned by tiny flaws in the crystal, the field is locked in place. That is why a magnet over a cold YBCO disk doesn’t just float but holds its position, even upside down: flux pinning.

03Stronger magnets

Double the field, shrink the reactor

Power per cubic metre—
Ring radius for the same power—
Magnet technology—

A tokamak’s magnetic field holds the plasma’s pressure. Double the field and the plasma can be four times denser; fusion power, which goes as the density squared, rises about sixteen times per cubic metre.

ITER uses niobium–tin superconductor cooled to 4 K by liquid helium, giving about 5.3 tesla at the plasma. High-temperature superconductors like REBCO (the YBCO family), made as thin tape, carry far more current in strong fields, and at a warmer 20 K. In 2021 MIT and Commonwealth Fusion Systems built a large magnet from this tape that reached 20 tesla.

That is how SPARC plans to match a big machine’s performance at around a fortieth of ITER’s plasma volume. A smaller machine is cheaper and quicker to build, which may matter more than anything for getting fusion onto the grid.

The math
P/V ∝ β² B⁴,  for fixed power V ∝ B⁻⁴ ⇒ R ∝ B−4/3

β is plasma pressure as a fraction of magnetic pressure, held fixed here, as is the machine’s shape. Real design is far more complex (confinement, heat exhaust, neutron shielding), but this simple scaling lands close to SPARC’s actual size: about 1.85 m major radius at 12.2 T versus ITER’s 6.2 m at 5.3 T. The field at the coils themselves is roughly twice that at the plasma.

Observations

Superconductors at work

Hospitals

MRI scanners

The biggest market for superconductors: coils of niobium–titanium wire in liquid helium, carrying a persistent current that keeps a 1.5–3 tesla field steady for years.

CERN

The Large Hadron Collider

1,232 superconducting dipole magnets at 1.9 K, each 15 m long, bend protons around a 27 km ring at 8.3 tesla.

Japan

SCMaglev

Superconducting magnets on the train levitate it 10 cm above the guideway. A test train reached 603 km/h in 2015; the Chūō Shinkansen line is being built.

2023

LK-99

A claimed room-temperature superconductor went viral. Within weeks, labs worldwide showed the effects came from copper sulphide impurities. The search for room temperature continues.
