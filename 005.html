entropy

THERMODYNAMICS · entropy — the number of ways things can be arranged that look the same from outside

Every law of motion works just as well backwards. Yet eggs break and never unbreak, and you remember yesterday but not tomorrow. The arrow of time isn’t written in the laws. It comes from counting, and from one very special starting point.

01Run it backwards

Why the egg never unbreaks

In the left quarter—
Run backwards—

Here are 320 particles bouncing off each other and the walls by Newton’s laws. They start packed into the left quarter of the box. Within seconds they fill it. That spreading is the second law of thermodynamics: entropy rises.

But Newton’s laws don’t care which way time runs. Press Reverse time and every particle’s velocity flips exactly. Played backwards, the laws are just as happy, and the gas un-spreads, gathering itself back into the corner like an egg reassembling.

So why does that never happen in real life? Try reversing after 1 second, then after 4. After a short run the gas finds its way home. After a long one it doesn’t. The computer rounds every number at the 16th decimal place, and each collision magnifies that tiny error, doubling it again and again. This is chaos. Within a few seconds the error is as big as the particles, and the road back is lost.

To unbreak an egg you would have to reverse every one of its trillions of trillions of molecules perfectly, with no error at all. Nature never sets that up. Disorder isn’t forced by the laws. It is just overwhelmingly easier to land in.

Try thisReverse at about 1 second: it returns. Reverse at 4 seconds: it fails. Then start again and use Reverse + nudge at 1 second. It flips time and moves one particle (circled) by a billionth of the box, and that alone ruins the return.
The math
S = kB ln W

Boltzmann’s entropy: W counts the microscopic arrangements that look the same from outside. The readout uses a coarse version: the Shannon entropy of how particles share 8 regions of the box, scaled so 100% means perfectly even.

δ(t) ≈ δ₀ eλt

In a chaotic system, a small difference δ grows exponentially at the Lyapunov rate λ. Starting from rounding errors of about 10⁻¹⁶, it takes only about 37/λ to reach full size. The model uses velocity Verlet integration with soft repulsive disks. In exact arithmetic it is perfectly time-reversible, so every failure to return is chaos amplifying rounding error. The puzzle is called Loschmidt’s paradox (1876).

02Counting

The second law is just very good odds

On the left now—
Chance all on one side—
Average wait for it—
Times seen0

Strip the physics down to pure chance. Each tick, one particle picks itself at random and hops to the other chamber. No forces, no pushing. Start with all of them on the left and watch.

They spread out to roughly half and half, and stay there. Nothing pushes them toward balance. There are simply far more ways to be balanced. With 10 particles, there is one way to have all 10 on the left but 252 ways to split them 5 and 5. That count of ways is what entropy measures.

With only 10 particles, the “impossible” happens. Every so often all of them wander back to one side, entropy drops, and for a moment the second law runs backwards. You can wait for it.

Raise the number and the odds collapse. At 100 particles you would wait longer than the age of the universe. A glass of water holds about 10²⁵ molecules. The second law isn’t strictly a law at all. It is a statistical certainty so strong that no one has ever seen it broken.

Try thisAt N = 10, watch the counter until all particles gather on one side. Then switch to 1,000 and see how the histogram turns into a narrow spike at half.
The math
W(n) = N!n! (N − n)!,  P(all on one side) = 21−N

This is the Ehrenfest urn model (1907). The long-run share of time spent with n particles on the left is the binomial distribution, the dashed curve. Its relative width shrinks like 1/√N. By Kac’s recurrence theorem, the average number of hops before all particles return to one side is 2N−1.

03The arrow

Guess which way time is running

Score0 of 0
This clipmake your guess
Arrow of time—

Each clip is a short movie made with the same laws as Bay 01, played either forwards or backwards. Every frame obeys Newton’s laws either way. Can you tell which way it runs?

Usually you can, and instantly. Colours mixing, a cluster spreading: forwards is the direction in which entropy grows. Your brain has learned the second law from every spilled drink you’ve ever seen.

Then you get a clip that is already fully mixed. Now there is no way to tell. Without a low-entropy starting point, there is no arrow of time, just particles jiggling.

So why does our universe have an arrow? Because it began in an extraordinarily ordered state, the smooth, hot, dense Big Bang. Physicists call this the Past Hypothesis. Roger Penrose estimated the odds of so special a start at 1 in 10 to the power 10¹²³. Every memory, every cause before its effect, every candle burning down is the universe still spreading out from that one ordered beginning.

Try thisPlay ten clips. Note which ones you could call and which you could only guess. That difference is the whole arrow of time.
The math
dSdt ≥ 0  (isolated system, from a low-entropy start)

The second law is not a theorem of the equations of motion, which are time-symmetric. It follows from them only together with a boundary condition: low entropy in the past. Given a typical state with no special past, entropy is overwhelmingly likely to be at or near its maximum in both time directions.

Observations

Entropy at the edges of physics

Information

Forgetting has a price

Erasing one bit of information must release at least kT ln 2 of heat. This is Landauer’s principle (1961), first measured directly in 2012. It explains why Maxwell’s demon, a tiny sorter that seems to beat the second law, can’t win: it has to forget.

Black holes

The most disordered objects there are

Bekenstein and Hawking showed that a black hole’s entropy is set by the area of its horizon, not its volume. A black hole holds more entropy than anything else of its size, a clue that space itself may be built from information.

Life

Order that pays its way

Living things build exquisite order, but only by exporting more disorder, as heat, to their surroundings. Erwin Schrödinger put it this way in his 1944 book What Is Life?: life feeds on low entropy, mostly sunlight.

The far future

Heat death

When entropy reaches its maximum, the arrow fades. Stars burn out, and even the largest black holes evaporate, within roughly 10¹⁰⁰ years. What remains is a cold, even, almost featureless universe in which nothing more can happen.

Note to self

The arrow of time isn’t in the laws of physics. It’s in the starting point, and in how many more ways there are to be messy than tidy.
