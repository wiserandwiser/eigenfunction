01One neuron

A single neuron draws one straight line

Verdict—

A neuron in a neural network is simple. It takes some numbers, multiplies each by a weight, adds them up with a bias, and squashes the total into a value between 0 and 1.

Feed it the position of a point (x, y) and something geometric happens. The neuron draws a straight line across the plane. On one side it answers “pink”, on the other “ice”, and the further from the line, the more confident it is. The weights set the line’s angle, and the bias shifts it.

Learning means nudging those three numbers to make fewer mistakes. Watch the line swing into place. Click to add points and it adjusts to them.

Now choose A ring. Pink in the middle, ice all around. No single straight line can separate them, so the neuron gives up and calls everything ice. In 1969 Minsky and Papert proved this limit for one-layer networks. The result helped bring on the first “AI winter”, until people learned to train networks with more layers.

Try thisScramble the weights and watch the line find its way back. Click a few pink points deep in ice territory and watch it struggle. Then try the ring.
The math
ŷ = σ(w₁x + w₂y + b),  σ(z) = 11 + e−z

The dividing line is where w₁x + w₂y + b = 0. The error is the cross-entropy loss, L = −[y ln ŷ + (1 − y) ln(1 − ŷ)], averaged over the points. Its slope with respect to each weight is simply (ŷ − y) times that weight’s input, which is what makes training cheap.

02How it learns

Learning is rolling downhill

Status—

How does a network know which way to nudge its weights? Take the simplest possible learner: a straight line with two numbers to tune, its slope and its intercept, fitted to some data points.

Every pair of numbers gives some error. Draw the error for every possible pair and you get a landscape: high ground where the line fits badly, a valley floor where it fits well. Learning is rolling downhill. At each step, measure which way the ground slopes (the gradient) and take a step that way. That is gradient descent, and it is how essentially every modern AI is trained.

The step size is the art. Too small and the ball creeps. Too big and it overshoots the valley and bounces from wall to wall. Bigger still and it flies off the map entirely.

Real networks don’t have two numbers. Large language models have billions, so their landscapes have billions of dimensions, full of ridges and plateaus. The bumpy landscape gives a flavour: the ball can get trapped in a small dip, and momentum, which lets it carry speed like a heavy ball, can help it roll through.

Try thisPush the step size up until the ball starts bouncing, then further until it diverges. Then switch to Bumpy, click on the map to drop the ball somewhere new, and compare with and without momentum.
The math
θ ← θ − η ∇L(θ)

Gradient descent: move the parameters θ = (slope, intercept) against the gradient of the loss, scaled by the step size (learning rate) η. Here L is the mean squared error. For this bowl the steepest direction has curvature 2, so the descent becomes unstable once η > 2/2 = 1. That is where you’ll see it diverge.

v ← βv − η ∇L,  θ ← θ + v

Momentum with β = 0.9. The bumpy landscape adds a made-up ripple, 0.12 sin(5m) cos(5b), to illustrate local minima.

A network learns a shape no line could draw

The fix for the ring was to add layers. Put a row of neurons between the input and the answer. Each neuron in that hidden layer draws its own line. The next layer combines those lines into corners, curves and pockets. Stack another layer and those curves combine into shapes as intricate as a spiral.

The small squares in the diagram show what each neuron “sees”: its own response across the whole plane. The first layer’s are all straight edges at different angles. Deeper ones are bent, built from those edges. Connections are pink when they excite and ice when they inhibit. Thicker means stronger.

Training is the downhill roll from Bay 02, now in a landscape with dozens of dimensions instead of two. The trick that makes it possible is backpropagation. The error at the output is passed backwards through the layers, telling every weight which way to move. It was popularised by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986, and it still trains every large AI model today.

Try thisWatch the spiral resolve. Then drop to 4 neurons per layer and restart: it often stalls. Too few neurons simply can’t bend that much. Try 1 layer on the checkerboard to see how few neurons a simpler shape needs.
The math
δ(l) = (W(l+1)⊤ δ(l+1)) ⊙ f ′(z(l)),  ∂L∂W(l) = δ(l) a(l−1)⊤

Backpropagation is the chain rule applied layer by layer, from the output back to the input. The model uses tanh hidden neurons, a sigmoid output, cross-entropy loss and the Adam optimiser (step size 0.03), training on all 240 points at every step. The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko, 1989) says one hidden layer with enough neurons can approximate any reasonable shape. In practice, depth gets there with far fewer neurons.

Observations

From this to today’s AI

2012 · ImageNet

The deep learning moment

A deep network called AlexNet, trained on graphics cards, cut the error rate in a major image-recognition contest by a wide margin. The same ingredients you see here, layers trained by backpropagation, suddenly beat decades of hand-built rules.

2017 · Transformers

Attention is all you need

The transformer architecture lets every word in a passage weigh every other word. It is the basis of today’s large language models, which are still trained by gradient descent, just on a vast scale.

2020 onwards · Scaling

Bigger, smoother, better

Researchers found that a language model’s error falls along a smooth, predictable curve as you add parameters, data and computing power. That finding has driven the race to train ever-larger models.

2024 · Nobel Prizes

Physics and chemistry honour AI

The physics prize went to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton for foundations of neural networks. The chemistry prize went to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for AlphaFold, which predicts protein structures, and to David Baker for protein design.
