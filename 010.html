entanglement

QUANTUM · entanglement — two particles sharing one state, so their answers stay linked however far apart they are

Measure one particle of an entangled pair and you instantly know something about its twin, light-years away. That alone could be pre-arranged, like a pair of gloves. In 1964 John Bell found a test that tells the difference, and nature fails the gloves.

01Linked answers

Two labs, one shared answer

Alice gets up—
Bob gets up—
Same answer—
Quantum prediction—

A source emits pairs of particles in a special shared spin state. One flies to Alice, the other to Bob. Each measures spin along an angle they choose, just like the analysers in Φ 009.

On their own, each person’s results look like coin tosses: up half the time, down half the time, with no pattern. But compare the two lists. With both dials at the same angle, the answers are always opposite. Every single time, at any distance.

Turn one dial and the link loosens smoothly. At 90° apart the answers agree half the time, and at 180° they always match.

Einstein called this “spooky action at a distance” and thought it meant quantum theory was incomplete. Perhaps each pair simply carries hidden instructions, decided at the source, like a pair of gloves split into two boxes. Open one, find a left glove, and you know the other is right. Nothing spooky. Bay 02 tests that idea.

Try thisSet both dials equal: always opposite. Now turn Bob’s dial and watch the “same answer” share climb along a smooth curve.
The math
|ψ⟩ = 1√2(|↑↓⟩ − |↓↑⟩),  P(same) = sin²(a − b2)

The singlet state. Neither particle has a spin of its own, only the pair does. The correlation between results is E(a, b) = −cos(a − b), where up = +1 and down = −1. The model draws each of Alice’s results at random and Bob’s from these joint odds.

02Bell’s test

A game that hidden instructions can’t win

Rounds played0
Bell score S—
Win rate—
Verdict—

Bell’s idea turns the experiment into a game. In each round, Alice randomly picks one of two angles and Bob randomly picks one of two angles, too late for any signal to pass between them. Then they compare results.

Combine the four correlations into a single number, the Bell score S. Bell proved that if each pair carries pre-set hidden instructions, however clever, S can never exceed 2. Equivalently, the CHSH version of the game can be won at most 75% of the time.

Switch the source to hidden instructions: each pair carries a secret angle that decides both answers, the best strategy of this kind. It tops out right at 2. Switch back to entangled pairs and the score climbs to about 2.83, winning 85% of rounds. No pre-arranged plan can do that.

Real experiments agree with quantum mechanics. In 2015 three groups (Delft, Vienna and Boulder) closed the remaining loopholes. The 2022 Nobel Prize went to Alain Aspect, John Clauser and Anton Zeilinger for this line of experiments. The particles have no pre-set answers, yet their answers stay linked.

Try thisLet a few thousand rounds run with each source. Watch the hidden-instruction score settle onto the line at 2 and never cross it, while entangled pairs sit well above.
The math
S = |E(a, b) + E(a, b′) + E(a′, b) − E(a′, b′)| ≤ 2

The CHSH inequality (1969), for any local hidden-variable model. Settings: a = 0°, a′ = 90°, b = 45°, b′ = −45°. Quantum mechanics gives 2√2 ≈ 2.83, Tsirelson’s bound. The win rate is ½ + S/8. The hidden-instruction model gives each pair a random angle λ, with A = sign cos(a − λ) and B = −sign cos(b − λ).

03No messages

Why you still can’t phone faster than light

Bob sees up—
Message Bob receives—
After comparing notes—

If Alice’s measurement instantly affects what Bob can find, can she use it to send him a message faster than light? Try it. Alice can choose her angle, or not measure at all. Bob, on his own, measures at 0°.

Whatever Alice does, Bob sees a perfectly random 50/50 stream. Her choice changes nothing he can detect. The link only shows up when they compare notes, and comparing notes needs an ordinary signal, travelling at light speed or slower.

So entanglement is real and nonlocal, but it can’t carry a message. Nature keeps the speed limit by making each side’s results look perfectly random on their own.

That randomness is useful. In 2017 China’s Micius satellite sent entangled photons to two ground stations 1,200 kilometres apart and confirmed the link still held. Φ 011 and 016 show how entanglement teleports quantum states and secures keys.

Bob’s own state is maximally mixed whatever Alice does: the no-signalling theorem. His odds of up are ½ along every axis. Information only appears in the joint statistics, which need classical communication to assemble.

Observations

From paradox to technology

1935 → 1964

EPR and Bell

Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen argued that entanglement showed quantum theory must be incomplete. Three decades later John Bell turned their philosophical argument into a number that could be measured.

2016 · The Big Bell Test

Settings chosen by people

About 100,000 volunteers worldwide played an online game, and their keystrokes chose the measurement settings in labs on five continents. Even with human choices, nature still violated Bell’s limit.

Space

Entanglement from orbit

The Micius satellite distributed entangled photon pairs over 1,200 kilometres. It is a step towards a quantum internet that links quantum computers and sensors.

Certified randomness

Randomness you can prove

A Bell violation guarantees the results weren’t pre-scripted. Labs now use this to generate random numbers whose unpredictability is certified by physics, not just trusted.

Note to self

Entangled particles have no answers of their own until measured, yet their answers stay linked. No hidden plan can match the link, and no message can ride on it.
