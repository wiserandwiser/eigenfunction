antimatter

PARTICLES & FORCES · mirror matter — every particle has a twin with the opposite charge; meet, and both turn into pure energy

In 1928 Paul Dirac wrote an equation for the electron that had a second, unexpected solution: a particle identical to the electron but with positive charge. Four years later it turned up in cosmic rays. Antimatter is now made routinely, used in hospital scanners every day, and poses one of the deepest puzzles in cosmology: why is there anything left at all?

01Matter from light

Pair creation in a magnetic field

Pair created?—
Energy each particle moves with—
Track radius at start—

E = mc² works both ways. A gamma-ray photon with enough energy, passing close to a nucleus, can turn into an electron and its antiparticle, the positron. Each has a mass worth 0.511 MeV, so the photon needs at least 1.022 MeV; anything extra becomes motion.

In a magnetic field, charged particles curve. The electron and positron have opposite charges, so their tracks curl in opposite directions, a signature Carl Anderson spotted in cosmic-ray photographs in 1932. As they lose energy to the material around them, their curves tighten into spirals.

When the positron finally slows down and meets an electron, the two annihilate: their mass turns back into two gamma rays of 0.511 MeV each, flying off in exactly opposite directions.

Try thisLower the energy below the threshold: nothing happens. Raise it and fire; then strengthen the field and watch the spirals tighten.
The math
Eγ ≥ 2mec² = 1.022 MeV,  r = p/(qB),  r[m] ≈ p[GeV/c] / (0.3 B[T])

The leftover energy is split equally here for simplicity (in reality the split varies). Tracks are drawn to scale for a chamber a few centimetres across, with a steady energy loss making them spiral. The nearby nucleus absorbs a little recoil, which is why pair creation can’t happen in empty space.

02Antimatter at work

The PET scanner

Annihilations recorded—
Hot spot found?—

A PET (positron emission tomography) scan uses antimatter on purpose. The patient is given a sugar-like molecule tagged with fluorine-18, a radioactive atom that emits positrons. Busy tissue, such as many cancers, soaks up more sugar and so glows brighter.

Each positron travels a millimetre or so, meets an electron and annihilates. The two gamma rays fly off back to back and hit a ring of detectors at almost the same instant. The annihilation happened somewhere on the line between those two hits.

One line tells you little. Tens of millions of them, overlapping, pile up where the tracer is concentrated. A computer combines them into a 3D image. Hospitals around the world run millions of these scans a year.

Try thisPress Scan and watch the lines accumulate. Switch to Image to see the hidden hot spot emerge.
The math
e⁺ + e⁻ → γ + γ,  Eγ = 511 keV each, back to back

The image here is a simple backprojection: every line adds a little brightness along its length, so a blurry halo surrounds each hot spot. Real scanners use filtered or iterative reconstruction, correct for absorption in the body, and use the slight difference in arrival times to locate each event along its line.

03A cosmic puzzle

Where did the antimatter go?

Survivors—
Light made per survivor—
In our universe—

The laws of physics treat matter and antimatter almost identically, so the Big Bang should have made equal amounts. As the universe cooled, every particle should have found its antiparticle and annihilated, leaving nothing but light.

Yet here we are. The microwave background (Φ 026) tells us there are about 1.6 billion photons for every proton. That fits a universe that started with roughly one extra particle of matter for every billion matched pairs. The pairs annihilated into light; the one-in-a-billion leftovers became every star, planet and person.

In 1967 Andrei Sakharov listed what that requires, including a difference in how matter and antimatter behave, called CP violation. It has been measured in quarks, but it is far too small to explain the leftover. The cause is one of the great open questions in physics.

Try thisSet the excess to zero and cool the universe: nothing survives. Then add a small excess and compare survivors with light.
The math
η = (nB − nB̄)/nγ ≈ 6 × 10⁻¹⁰

The baryon-to-photon ratio, measured from the cosmic microwave background and from the amounts of light elements made in the first minutes. The animation exaggerates the excess enormously (percent instead of parts per billion) so that a few survivors are visible among a few hundred particles.

Observations

Milestones

1932

The positron

Carl Anderson photographed a cosmic-ray track that curved the wrong way for an electron but was far too light for a proton. Nobel Prize, 1936.

1955

The antiproton

Emilio Segrè and Owen Chamberlain made antiprotons at Berkeley’s Bevatron, built with just enough energy to create them.

1995 · 2010

Antihydrogen

CERN made the first antihydrogen atoms in 1995; in 2010 the ALPHA experiment trapped them in magnetic bottles for the first time.

2023

Antimatter falls down

ALPHA-g showed that antihydrogen falls under gravity like ordinary matter, ruling out the idea that antimatter is repelled by Earth.

Note to self

Energy can become a particle and its antiparticle, and they annihilate back into light; why a sliver of matter survived the Big Bang is still unknown.
