reincarnation research

FRINGE SCIENCE · cases of the reincarnation type — young children who describe, often in verifiable detail, the life of a person who died before they were born

From 1961 until his death in 2007, the psychiatrist Ian Stevenson, chair of psychiatry at the University of Virginia, travelled the world investigating children, usually aged two to five, who described memories of another life. He collected some 2,500 cases with painstaking interviews, records and post-mortem reports, and his work continues today at the university’s Division of Perceptual Studies under Jim Tucker. The best cases contain dozens of specific, verified details. Here you can see how fast specifics shrink the odds of a chance match, test birthmarks against wounds, and look at how the previous lives ended.

01Written before the meeting

How many people could fit?

People who fit by chance—
Chance that anyone fits—
Statements in the strongest cases—

A typical case begins when a toddler says things like “my name was Gopal”, “I lived in Mathura”, “my brother shot me”, “we owned a medicine company”. Often, in a few dozen cases, the statements were written down by a researcher or the family before anyone went looking for a matching person.

How surprising a match is depends on how specific the statements are. “I had a car” fits millions; a name, a town, a business and a manner of death together fit almost nobody by chance.

The case forIn the strongest cases, Stevenson and later the Icelandic psychologist Erlendur Haraldsson documented dozens of specific statements, including names, places and relationships, written down before the previous family was found, with most of them correct. When Sybo Schouten and Stevenson compared cases recorded before verification with those recorded afterwards in 1998, accuracy was similar, roughly three statements in four correct, suggesting that late recording had not inflated the hits. Children also recognised people and places from the previous life, and often showed behaviours, skills and emotions fitting it, which chance matching of statements cannot explain.
The case againstCases written down before a match are a small minority; in most, the families had already met, and memories of what the child said may have been reshaped afterwards.
Try thisAdd specific statements one at a time and watch the crowd of possible matches empty. Then make the search area larger: even a whole country barely helps chance once there are eight specifics.
The math
expected chance matches = N × Π pi

Each specific statement is given a probability of fitting a random person, from about one in two for broad details to about one in fifty for a name. N is the number of people who died in the searched area over the relevant years.

02Reincarnation and Biology, 1997

Two marks for two wounds

One mark near one wound, by chance—
Two marks matching entry and exit—
Double-mark cases Stevenson reported—

Stevenson’s two-volume, 2,268-page Reincarnation and Biology described more than 200 children with birthmarks or birth defects that corresponded to wounds, often fatal ones, on the person whose life they recalled. Where he could, he obtained post-mortem reports and hospital records to confirm the wounds.

Birthmarks are common, so one mark near one wound proves little. But a bullet usually leaves two wounds: a small round entry and a larger, irregular exit on the opposite side of the body.

The case forStevenson reported 18 cases in which children had two birthmarks, one small and round and one larger and irregular, matching the entry and exit wounds of the person they described, confirmed in several cases by medical documents. Two marks, each in the right place and with the right shapes, are vastly less likely by chance than one. Some children also had birth defects, such as missing fingers, matching injuries that are almost never seen as congenital conditions.
The case againstIn many cases the marks were linked to wounds only after a previous person had been identified, and how close counts as a match was not fixed in advance.
Try thisDraw several random children and see how often a single mark lands near a wound. Then look at the odds of both wounds being matched at once, and tighten the matching distance.
The math
P(both) ≈ P(entry matched) × P(exit matched)

Each simulated child has three birthmarks placed at random on the front or back of the body. A wound counts as matched if a mark lies within the chosen distance on the right side of the body. The chance figures come from 20,000 simulated children.

03How the previous lives ended

A pattern no one expected

Violent deaths among previous persons—
Violent deaths in the population—
Over-representation—

If children’s stories were fantasy shaped by family culture, the lives they describe might be expected to be ordinary or idealised. Instead, the previous persons in these cases died violently far more often than people in general: murdered, drowned, killed in accidents or in war.

Many of the children also showed fears that fit the death they described, such as a fear of water in a child who recalled drowning, often before they could explain why.

The case forAcross the University of Virginia cases, about 70% of the previous persons died by unnatural means, according to Jim Tucker, compared with a few percent of deaths in the general population. Stevenson found that about a third of children who described a violent death had a phobia related to its mode, sometimes from infancy. Proponents argue that a pattern this strong and this specific is hard to produce from imagination or family suggestion.
The case againstViolent deaths are more talked about in a community and more likely to be connected to a child’s statements, so cases may be selected for them.

The math
over-representation = share in cases / share in population

Milestones

1966

Twenty Cases

Stevenson’s Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation set out his method of detailed interviews in India, Sri Lanka, Brazil, Lebanon and Alaska.

1977

A medical journal

Stevenson’s work appeared in the Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease, where psychiatrist Harold Lief wrote that he would be known either for a colossal mistake or as “the Galileo of the 20th century”.

1997

Reincarnation and Biology

Stevenson’s two-volume study of birthmarks and birth defects linked to the deaths of previous persons.

2005–

Life Before Life

Jim Tucker’s books brought the cases to a wide audience and added detailed cases from the United States.

Note to self

Specific statements recorded in advance, double birthmarks matching wounds and a striking excess of violent deaths: the best cases are, in Stevenson’s words, suggestive of reincarnation.
