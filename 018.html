where gold comes from

ASTROPHYSICS · cosmic chemistry — every atom heavier than helium was cooked somewhere, and the ring on your finger came from a collision of dead stars

The Big Bang made hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. Everything else, the carbon in you, the iron in your blood, the gold in a wedding ring, was made later inside stars or in the violence of their deaths. Each element has a birthplace, and astronomers can now map them.

01The fuel ledger

Why a star’s forge stops at iron

Burning—
Pays out (vs hydrogen)—
Burns needed to stay up—

A star shines by turning light nuclei into heavier ones. Each step releases a little energy, because the new nucleus is bound together more tightly than its ingredients, and the difference escapes as heat and light.

The first step is by far the best deal. Hydrogen to helium releases about ten times more energy per kilogram than any later stage. Every stage after that, helium, carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon, pays out less, so the core has to burn its fuel faster just to keep holding the star up. That is why the last stages in Φ 017 last months, then days.

The nuclei around iron are the most tightly bound of all. There is no more energy to win by building with them: making anything heavier costs energy overall. So the stellar forge stops at iron, and the iron core collapses. Every element heavier than iron had to be made a different way.

The math
energy released ≈ Δ(binding energy) per nucleon:  H→He 6.7 MeV, He→C,O ~0.7, C ~0.4, Ne ~0.1, O ~0.3, Si→Fe ~0.2

Approximate values per nucleon from measured nuclear masses. Hydrogen burning turns about 0.7% of the fuel’s mass into energy; all the later stages together add only about a quarter more. “Burns needed” is the rate of fusions required to supply the same power, relative to hydrogen burning; in reality the late stages also lose heavily to neutrinos, which makes the squeeze worse.

02Beyond iron

Two roads: the slow and the rapid

Where—
Time between neutron grabs—
Mass 197 settles as—

Beyond iron, nuclei grow by catching neutrons. A neutron has no charge, so it is not repelled by the nucleus. Each catch makes the nucleus heavier; when it has too many neutrons, one turns into a proton, and the nucleus becomes the next element up. On this chart that is a step right, then a step diagonally up and left.

The slow road runs inside ageing giant stars, which leak a gentle trickle of neutrons. A nucleus waits years to thousands of years between catches, so it always has time to settle, and the path creeps along the band of stable nuclei. It makes about half the heavy elements, including barium and lead, and it dead-ends at bismuth.

The rapid road needs a flood of neutrons, so many that a nucleus catches dozens in under a second, faster than it can decay. When two neutron stars spiral together and merge, they spray out neutron-rich debris that does exactly this. The path races far to the right, stalls at certain extra-stable neutron counts, then, as the debris thins, every nucleus decays back to the stable band. That is how most of the universe’s gold, platinum and europium were made.

τcapture ≫ τβ (slow)    τcapture ≪ τβ (rapid)

The slow process needs about 10⁷–10⁸ free neutrons per cm³; the rapid process needs more than 10²⁰. The rapid path pauses at the “magic” neutron numbers 50, 82 and 126, which is why the universe has abundance peaks near masses 80, 130 and 195. Paths and the stable band here come from a simplified model; real paths depend on measured masses and decay rates of very short-lived nuclei.

03The cosmic periodic table

Every element has a birthplace

Element—
Made mainly in—
Story—

Put the two lessons together and you can colour the periodic table by origin. The light elements of life, carbon, oxygen and nitrogen, come from ordinary stars: puffed off by dying Sun-like stars or blasted out in supernovae. Much of the iron comes from white dwarfs that explode after stealing gas from a companion.

Further down the table, the slow road in giant stars and the rapid road in neutron-star mergers share the work. Gold, platinum and europium are almost entirely rapid-road products.

On 17 August 2017, gravitational-wave detectors heard two neutron stars merge 130 million light-years away (Φ 019). Telescopes then watched the glow of the debris, a kilonova, redden over days as heavy elements formed in it. By some estimates, it made several Earth masses of gold.

Observations

How we found out

1952

Technetium in red giants

Paul Merrill found technetium in the light of red giant stars. It has no stable form and decays within a few million years, so the stars must be making it right now: the first proof that stars forge elements.

1957

B²FH

Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle wrote the 100-page paper that laid out how stars build the elements, naming the slow and rapid processes still used today.

2016

Reticulum II

A faint dwarf galaxy whose old stars are loaded with europium and other rapid-road elements, seeded, it seems, by a single rare event such as a neutron-star merger.

2017

GW170817

The first neutron-star merger seen in gravitational waves and light together. Its kilonova was the first time astronomers watched heavy elements being made, and in 2019 strontium was identified in its spectrum.

Note to self

Stars forge the elements up to iron; beyond that, nuclei grow by catching neutrons, slowly in giant stars or in a flash when neutron stars collide.
