quark confinement

PARTICLES & FORCES · the strong force — a force that grows stronger the further you pull, so quarks can never be set free

Nobody has ever seen a single quark on its own, and nobody ever will. Pull two quarks apart and the force between them does not weaken like gravity or magnetism: it stays as strong as a sixteen-tonne weight, until the energy you’ve put in snaps the bond and creates new quarks. Only by heating matter to trillions of degrees do quarks briefly roam free.

01The unbreakable string

Pull two quarks apart

Energy stored—
Force pulling back—
State—

Between two electric charges, field lines spread out through all of space, so the force weakens with distance. Gluons are different: they carry colour charge themselves, so they attract each other. The field between two quarks squeezes into a narrow flux tube, like a string.

A string of constant thickness pulls with a constant force, however far you stretch it: about 1 GeV per femtometre, the weight of roughly sixteen tonnes, acting across a distance smaller than a proton. The energy stored grows steadily as you pull.

At about a femtometre and a bit, there is enough energy in the string to make a new quark and antiquark from nothing (E = mc²). The string snaps, and each broken end is capped by a new quark. You don’t get a free quark; you get two particles where there was one.

The math
V(r) = −(4/3) αs ħc/r + σr,  σ ≈ 0.9 GeV/fm ≈ 1.4 × 10⁵ N

The Cornell potential, which fits the measured masses of charm–anticharm mesons well; αs ≈ 0.3 here. The string breaks when the stored energy can pay for a new pair of light mesons, around 1.2–1.4 fm in lattice calculations; here it breaks once about 1 GeV has been stored beyond a normal meson’s size. The electric comparison is a Coulomb potential scaled to the same short-range strength. 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.

02Jets

Collide, and the strings shatter into sprays

Jets—
Charged particles (typical)—

In a collider, an electron and a positron annihilate and their energy creates a quark and an antiquark flying apart at nearly the speed of light. The string between them stretches, snaps, the new ends fly apart and snap again, over and over.

Within about 10⁻²³ seconds the original pair has become a dozen or more ordinary particles, mostly pions, travelling in two tight sprays: jets. Detectors never see the quarks themselves, only the jets pointing back along their paths.

Sometimes a quark shakes off a high-energy gluon, which makes its own jet. In 1979 the PETRA collider in Hamburg saw these three-jet events, the first direct evidence that gluons exist.

03Melting the proton

Quark–gluon plasma

Temperature—
State—
Where this happens—

The strong force weakens at short distances and high energies (Φ 035). Heat nuclear matter enough and the particles crowd so close and move so fast that the strings dissolve. Protons and neutrons melt into a soup of free quarks and gluons: the quark–gluon plasma.

The changeover happens at about 155 MeV, roughly two trillion degrees, a hundred thousand times hotter than the Sun’s core. The whole universe was this hot for its first few microseconds, before quarks condensed into protons and neutrons.

Colliders recreate it by smashing gold or lead nuclei together. In 2005 Brookhaven’s RHIC found the plasma behaves not like a gas but like an almost perfect liquid, flowing with less friction than any other known substance.

The math
Tc ≈ 155 MeV ≈ 1.8 × 10¹² K,  η/s ≈ 1/4π (near the lower bound)

Lattice QCD shows that at near-zero density the change is a smooth crossover, not a sharp boiling point. η/s is the ratio of viscosity to entropy density, which in the plasma comes close to a theoretical lower limit. The animation is schematic: quarks drift and rebind with a probability that falls as temperature rises.

Observations

Milestones

1973

Asymptotic freedom

David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer showed that the strong force weakens at high energy, making QCD calculable there. Nobel Prize, 2004.

1979

The gluon

Experiments at DESY’s PETRA collider in Hamburg recorded three-jet events, the first direct sight of gluons.

2005

The perfect liquid

Brookhaven’s Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider reported that quark–gluon plasma flows like a near-frictionless liquid; the LHC confirmed it with lead nuclei.

Open

The Millennium Prize

Proving mathematically that confinement follows from the theory (the Yang–Mills mass gap) is one of the Clay Institute’s seven $1 million problems, still unsolved.
