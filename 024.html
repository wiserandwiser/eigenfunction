dark matter

ASTROPHYSICS · cosmology — most of the matter in the universe gives off no light, and we know it is there only by its pull

Weigh a galaxy by its stars and gas, then weigh it by how fast it spins, and the answers disagree by a factor of five or more. The same gap shows up in galaxy clusters, in bent light and in the afterglow of the Big Bang. Something unseen outweighs all the atoms in the cosmos, and nobody yet knows what it is.

01Spin speed

The outer stars move too fast

Dark : visible mass within 25 kpc—

The planets far from the Sun orbit slowly: Neptune at 5 km/s, Earth at 30. That is what you expect when nearly all the mass sits in the middle. A galaxy’s light is also concentrated towards its centre, so its outer stars should orbit slowly too.

In the 1970s Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars and gas orbit in spiral galaxies. Far out, where the light fades away, the speeds don’t drop. The curves stay flat to the edge and beyond, traced by radio waves from hydrogen gas.

Flat curves mean the mass inside each orbit keeps growing with distance, long after the stars run out. Each galaxy must sit inside a huge, invisible halo, several times heavier than everything we can see.

02Weighing with light

A cluster of galaxies bends the light behind it

Mass included—
Einstein ring radius—

Mass curves space, so light passing a heavy object bends (Φ 003). A galaxy cluster, hundreds of galaxies bound together, acts as a giant, imperfect lens: galaxies far behind it are stretched into arcs, and sometimes into near-complete rings.

How strongly the light bends measures all the mass, visible or not. Add up the stars and the hot gas in a cluster and they bend light far too weakly to make the arcs we see. The lensing demands roughly six times more mass.

Fritz Zwicky first suspected missing mass in 1933, when galaxies in the Coma cluster turned out to be moving far too fast to be held together by what could be seen. He called it dunkle Materie.

03The smoking gun

When two clusters collide, the mass and the gas separate

Most of the normal matter is—
Most of the mass is—

Most of the normal matter in a cluster is not in its galaxies but in hot gas between them, at tens of millions of degrees, glowing in X-rays. The galaxies themselves are tiny targets, and when two clusters collide they sail past each other. The gas clouds can’t: they slam together and are slowed, like two water-balloons hitting.

In the Bullet Cluster, two clusters collided about 150 million years before the light we now see left them. The X-ray gas, pink, is stuck in the middle. Lensing reveals where the mass is, blue, and it has carried on through with the galaxies, away from most of the normal matter.

If the extra gravity came from some change to the law of gravity acting on normal matter, it should follow the gas. It doesn’t. Whatever dark matter is, it barely interacts, even with itself.

Observations

What could it be?

Candidate

WIMPs

Heavy particles that interact only weakly. Huge tanks of liquid xenon deep underground, like LZ in South Dakota, wait for one to nudge an atom. None has been seen yet, ruling out much of the simplest range.

Candidate

Axions

Extremely light particles proposed to fix a puzzle in nuclear physics. In a strong magnetic field they might turn into faint radio waves; experiments like ADMX tune through frequencies listening for them.

Candidate

Primordial black holes

Black holes formed in the first second of the Big Bang. Lensing surveys and gravitational waves have ruled them out across most mass ranges, but a few windows remain.

Alternative

Modified gravity

MOND changes the law of gravity at very low accelerations and fits many galaxy rotation curves well. It struggles with clusters, the Bullet Cluster and the cosmic microwave background.

Note to self

About 85% of all matter is dark: it pulls on stars and bends light but gives off nothing, and what it is remains one of the biggest open questions in physics.
