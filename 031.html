BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · computing — a hundred billion switches on a fingernail, printed with light, and now meeting quantum mechanics head-on

Every computer is built from transistors, tiny switches that turn a current on and off. For 60 years engineers have halved their size every few years, and a top chip now holds over 100 billion of them. But the smallest parts are only a few dozen atoms across, where electrons start to tunnel straight through a switch that is meant to be off.

01The switch

Shrink a transistor until it leaks

Switch is—
On ÷ off current—
Off-state leakage mostly by—

A transistor is a channel of silicon between a source and a drain, with a gate electrode above it. Electrons in the source face an energy barrier. Voltage on the gate lowers the barrier and electrons pour over: the switch is on. Remove it and the barrier rises: off.

Even when “off”, a few electrons have enough thermal energy to get over, which sets a hard limit on how sharply a transistor can switch at room temperature. As the channel shortens, the drain starts to pull the barrier down too, and the gate loses control. Below about 5 nanometres a new leak opens: electrons tunnel straight through the barrier (Φ 002).

The industry’s answer has been to wrap the gate around the channel. First the FinFET (2011), a fin of silicon gripped on three sides; now gate-all-around nanosheets (2022 onwards), gripped on all four. Each grip gives the gate firmer control at shorter lengths.

A teaching model: over-the-barrier current plus WKB tunnelling through it, with gate control α and a “natural length” λ that shrinks as the gate wraps further round (planar 12 nm, FinFET 6 nm, gate-all-around 4 nm). The thermal limit is 60 mV of gate voltage per tenfold change in current at room temperature. Chip makers want on/off ratios above about 10⁵.

02Moore’s law

Fifty years of doubling

Growth since the Intel 4004—

In 1965 Gordon Moore noticed that the number of components on a chip was doubling every year; in 1975 he revised it to every two years. That observation became a target the whole industry planned around, and it held for half a century.

Intel’s 4004 of 1971 had 2,300 transistors. Today’s largest chips have tens to hundreds of billions, a growth of over ten million times. No other technology has improved this fast for this long.

Node names like “3 nm” no longer measure anything physical. The tightest gate spacing on such a chip is about 45–50 nm, and the smallest metal wires sit about 20–24 nm apart. Density still improves, but more slowly and at rising cost. Much of the progress now comes from stacking chips and packaging several dies together.

03Printing with light

Extreme ultraviolet lithography

Finest pitch this light can print—
Image contrast—
Result—

Chips are printed like photographs. Light shines through a mask, a lens shrinks the pattern, and it exposes a light-sensitive coating on the silicon wafer. The finest detail a lens can form is limited by diffraction: roughly the wavelength divided by the lens’s light-gathering power, its numerical aperture (NA).

For two decades the industry squeezed 193 nm light with every trick: immersing the lens in water to raise NA, and exposing each layer two or four times. Then came extreme ultraviolet at 13.5 nm. Making it means hitting 50,000 falling droplets of molten tin per second with a powerful laser, turning each into a plasma hotter than the Sun’s surface.

EUV is absorbed by almost everything, air and glass included, so the whole machine runs in a vacuum and focuses with mirrors made of alternating layers of molybdenum and silicon. Only the Dutch company ASML makes these machines; each costs a couple of hundred million dollars, and the new High-NA versions roughly twice that.

Observations

Milestones

1947

The first transistor

John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley at Bell Labs built a point-contact transistor from germanium. Nobel Prize, 1956.

1958–59

The integrated circuit

Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce independently put several components on one piece of semiconductor, the start of the microchip.

2019

EUV in production

After more than two decades of development, EUV lithography entered mass production for the most advanced chips, first in smartphones.

Next

Stacking

Chip makers are moving into the third dimension: stacking memory on logic, bonding dies face to face, and planning transistors stacked on top of each other.
