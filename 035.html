PARTICLES & FORCES · fundamental physics — seventeen particles and three forces that explain everything we’ve ever measured, except gravity

Everything you have ever touched is made of just three particles: up quarks, down quarks and electrons. Add a handful of heavier cousins, a few force-carrying particles and the Higgs, and you have the Standard Model, the most precisely tested theory in science. This lesson is a map of the whole thing.

Seventeen particles

The matter particles, fermions, come in two families. Quarks feel the strong force and are always locked inside larger particles. Leptons, like the electron and the ghostly neutrinos, do not.

Both come in three generations, each a heavier copy of the one before. Ordinary matter uses only the first. The muon, discovered in 1936, prompted Isidor Rabi to ask “Who ordered that?”, and nobody yet knows why nature repeats itself three times.

The bosons carry the forces: the photon for electromagnetism, eight gluons for the strong force, and the W and Z for the weak force that powers radioactive decay. The Higgs boson, found in 2012, is the visible ripple of the field that gives particles their mass (Φ 036). Their masses span more than twelve powers of ten, from neutrinos under an electronvolt to the top quark, as heavy as a gold atom.

02Building matter

Make a proton from quarks

You made—
Charge—
Mass from the quarks themselves—

Quarks have charges in thirds: an up quark carries +⅔, a down quark −⅓. Two ups and a down make +1, the proton; one up and two downs make 0, the neutron. Every atomic nucleus is built from these two.

Quarks also carry a second kind of charge, called colour: red, green or blue (nothing to do with real colours). Only colour-neutral combinations exist on their own: three quarks, one of each colour, or a quark paired with an antiquark of the matching anticolour.

The surprise is the mass. The three quarks in a proton add up to about 9 MeV, but the proton weighs 938 MeV. About 99% of its mass is the energy of the gluon field and the frantic motion binding the quarks together (E = mc²). Most of the mass of your body is bound-up energy.

03The forces

Strengths that change with energy

Strong—
Weak—
Electromagnetic—

Forces act by exchanging particles: two electrons repel by trading photons, quarks stick together by trading gluons. The strength of each force is set by a number, its coupling. At everyday energies the strong force is about a hundred times stronger than electromagnetism, which in turn is far stronger than the weak force.

But couplings change with energy. Empty space is a froth of short-lived virtual particles. Around an electron they screen its charge, so electromagnetism grows stronger as you probe closer. Gluons, which carry colour themselves, have the opposite effect: the strong force grows weaker at high energy. This is asymptotic freedom, which won the 2004 Nobel Prize.

Run the three forward and they approach each other around 1015–1016 GeV, as if they were once a single force. In the Standard Model alone they just miss. Whether a deeper theory makes them meet is one of physics’ big open questions.

Observations

Milestones

1897

The electron

J. J. Thomson found that cathode rays were made of tiny charged particles, the first elementary particle discovered.

1964–1968

Quarks

Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig proposed quarks to bring order to the particle zoo. Electron-scattering experiments at SLAC found point-like particles inside the proton in 1968.

1983

W and Z

CERN’s proton–antiproton collider found the carriers of the weak force at the predicted masses, confirming electroweak unification.

1995 · 2012

Top and Higgs

Fermilab found the top quark in 1995. CERN’s Large Hadron Collider found the Higgs boson in 2012, completing the Standard Model.
