FRINGE SCIENCE · the Global Consciousness Project — a worldwide network of random number generators testing whether mass human attention leaves a trace in physical randomness

In 1998 Roger Nelson, formerly of Princeton’s PEAR lab, set up random number generators in dozens of countries, each producing a stream of random bits every second. The hypothesis: when millions of people focus on the same event, a funeral, a disaster, New Year’s Eve, the generators everywhere should become slightly less random together. Every test was registered before the data were examined. After 17 years and about 500 events, the combined result stood at odds against chance of around a trillion to one. Here you can watch the network on 11 September 2001, see distant devices fall into step, and add up the whole formal series.

Each generator, nicknamed an “egg”, produces 200 random bits a second. Every second the project combines all the eggs into one number measuring how far they stray from chance together. On an ordinary day the running total wanders like a random walk inside the chance band.

On 11 September 2001 the eggs were running in about 40 locations around the world when the first plane struck at 8:46 in New York.

The case forThe GCP reported that the network deviated strongly in the hours around the attacks, one of the largest deviations in its record, and published the analysis in Foundations of Physics Letters in 2002. An independent analysis by Dean Radin, using a different statistic, also found a marked departure from chance that day. The timing matters: the deviation followed the attacks, not the other way round, and on such a day the attention of much of humanity was fixed on a single event.
The case againstResearchers including Edwin May and James Spottiswoode found that the 9/11 result depended on the chosen window and statistic, and other reasonable choices gave weaker results.
Try thisReplay the day and watch where the line leaves the chance band. Then set the effect to zero and replay: an ordinary day stays inside the band.
The math
network variance each second: Zs² − 1, summed over time

Each second the eggs’ z-scores are combined (Stouffer) and squared; with pure chance the running sum of Z² − 1 wanders around zero. The effect you set adds a small excess variance during the hours after 8:46, scaled from the published curve.

Distant eggs fall into step

If mass attention affects randomness, what exactly changes? In 2008 Peter Bancel and Roger Nelson took the formal results apart in the Journal of Scientific Exploration to look for the structure inside them.

They found that the effect does not live inside single eggs, each of which stays close to random on its own. It appears as small correlations between pairs of eggs, often on different continents, during events, as if separated devices were responding to something they share.

The case forPhysical devices thousands of kilometres apart, built from independent electronic noise sources and shielded from each other, should never correlate. Bancel and Nelson reported that during events they did, consistently across the formal series, and that the effect showed in independent statistics computed from the same data, which is hard to explain by a single lucky choice of analysis.
The case againstBancel later concluded, in 2017, that the overall pattern fits subtle selection by the experimenters better than a global effect.
Try thisCompare events with ordinary days and count the linked pairs. Lower the effect and watch the lines thin to what chance alone would give.
The math
pair correlation rij = mean(zi × zj)

A pair is drawn when its correlation is beyond the 95% chance range, so about 5% of pairs light up even with no effect. The effect adds a small shared signal to every egg during events. Locations are schematic.

Adding up 500 events

The project’s headline result combined all its formal tests. For each event, the start, end and statistic were written into a public registry before anyone looked at the data, the same safeguard used in clinical trials.

Individually most events showed only a small deviation. Added together, about 500 events gave a combined score near 7 standard deviations: odds against chance of roughly a trillion to one.

The case forA result this far from chance, built from pre-registered tests over 17 years, is among the strongest datasets in parapsychology. The effect is small and steady, an average shift of about a third of a standard deviation per event, and it accumulated across hundreds of very different events, from meditations to tragedies, exactly as a real but subtle influence would. No one has identified an ordinary physical cause, and the eggs behave as random in calibration runs.
The case againstThe combined score shows something shifted the numbers, not what; choosing which events to test still involved human judgment, and the effect did not clearly grow with the size of an event’s audience.
Try thisRerun the series at a shift of 0.33 and watch the line climb out of the chance band within a few years. Set the shift to zero and it stays inside.
The math
cumulative Z = Σzi / √n,  mean shift 0.33 over 500 events → Z ≈ 0.33 × √500 ≈ 7.4

Each simulated event contributes a z-score drawn from a normal distribution shifted by the amount you set. The band shows where 95% of pure-chance series would be.
