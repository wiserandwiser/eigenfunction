orgone energy

FRINGE SCIENCE · Wilhelm Reich’s orgone — a proposed universal life energy, said to collect inside layered boxes and to act on the body and the weather

Wilhelm Reich was a pupil of Freud and one of the most influential psychoanalysts of his generation. In the late 1930s he announced a new energy, orgone, present in living things and the atmosphere, and built “accumulators” of alternating organic and metal layers to concentrate it. Albert Einstein tested one himself. Reich’s story ended in a US prison in 1957 after a court ordered his books burned, an episode his supporters still see as persecution rather than refutation. His followers have continued measuring. Here are the temperature anomaly Einstein saw, a blinded study of the body, and the cloudbuster.

Einstein checks the box

Reich’s name for it—

Reich reported that a thermometer above the top of an accumulator read slightly warmer than the surrounding air, with no source of heat inside. He called the difference To − T and took it as the energy concentrating.

In January 1941 he visited Albert Einstein at Princeton and explained the idea in a five-hour conversation. Einstein agreed that a heat source in an inert box would be a “bombshell” for physics, took a small accumulator home, and confirmed the temperature difference.

The case forEinstein saw the effect himself. When his assistant suggested convection in the room, Reich replied with a 25-page letter of new controls: the difference persisted with the box suspended, in the open air, and against an identical box without metal layers. In later decades the researcher James DeMeo reported the same small warming, around a few tenths of a degree, in controlled comparisons with sham enclosures.
The case againstEinstein concluded the difference came from the room’s temperature gradient and stopped corresponding, and side-by-side tests at matched heights are needed to rule out convection.

02Marburg, 1980s

A blinded test on the body

Change after the real accumulator—
Change after the sham box—
Design—

The fairest way to test a claimed bodily effect is to blind it: volunteers sit in boxes that look and feel identical, and neither they nor the person recording knows which one contains the metal layers.

In the late 1980s Stefan Müschenich and Rainer Gebauer ran such a study at the University of Marburg in Germany.

The case forIn a double-blind design, the Marburg study reported measurable physiological differences after sitting in the real accumulator compared with the sham box, including changes in skin temperature, pulse and blood pressure, and more frequent reports of warmth and tingling. Since neither volunteers nor experimenters knew which box was which, expectation alone should not produce a difference.
The case againstThe study has not been independently replicated, and no rigorous trial has shown that accumulators treat any disease; US regulators in the 1950s found no evidence they did.

03The cloudbuster

Rain after the operations

Operations this season—

In the 1950s Reich built the “cloudbuster”, a set of long metal tubes grounded in water, which he said could draw orgone from the sky and trigger rain. During a severe drought in Maine in 1953 local farmers paid him to try, and rain followed.

The geographer James DeMeo has continued the work, running operations in drought regions of the United States, Africa and the Middle East.

The case forReich’s 1953 Maine operation was followed within hours by rain that saved the blueberry crop, as local newspapers reported. DeMeo has published comparisons showing more rain in the days after operations than in comparable periods, including in Arizona and in drought-stricken Eritrea in the 1990s. Cloud seeding shows that modest human interventions can change rainfall, so the idea is not absurd in principle.
The case againstNo randomised, independently monitored trial has shown an effect, and even cloud seeding, with a known mechanism, needs carefully randomised trials to detect its modest influence.

Einstein’s test

After a five-hour meeting, Einstein tested an accumulator for about two weeks and confirmed the temperature difference.

Prison

Reich was jailed for contempt of court, and his books and journals were burned by order of the court; he died in prison in 1957.
