the oldest light

ASTROPHYSICS · the cosmic microwave background — a baby picture of the universe, taken 380,000 years after the Big Bang

Point a radio telescope at any patch of empty sky and you pick up a faint microwave glow, the same in every direction to one part in 100,000. It is light released when the young universe first turned transparent, stretched by 13.8 billion years of expansion. Its tiny ripples record what the universe is made of, and its shape.

01The fog clears

When electrons settle into atoms, light flies free

Universe age—
Electrons still free—
Light can travel—

For its first few hundred thousand years the universe was a hot, glowing plasma: protons and electrons not yet bound into atoms. Light can’t get far in a plasma, because free electrons scatter it in all directions. The universe was opaque, like the inside of a cloud.

As space expanded the plasma cooled. Around 3,000 K, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, electrons settled onto protons to make neutral hydrogen. Atoms barely scatter that light, so almost all at once the fog cleared and the light flew free.

That light has been travelling ever since. When we look at it, we see the “surface” where the fog lifted, the farthest and oldest thing we can see with light.

The math
x²/(1 − x) = (1/nb) (mekT/2πħ²)3/2 e−13.6 eV/kT

The Saha equation gives the fraction x of free electrons at temperature T. It turns over near 3,700 K, even though 13.6 eV corresponds to 158,000 K: there are about 1.6 billion photons for every proton, and enough of them are energetic enough to break atoms apart until it is much cooler. The full calculation (Peebles, 1968) lands near 3,000 K. Ages follow from T = 2.725 K × (1 + z) in a standard model.

02Stretched light

From an orange glow to a microwave hiss

Temperature—
Brightest wavelength—
Space has stretched by—

At release the light had the spectrum of a 3,000 K glowing body, a dull orange-red with most of its energy in the near infrared. As space expanded, every wavelength stretched with it. Space has grown about 1,100 times since, so the light is now microwaves, peaking at about a millimetre, and the glow has cooled to 2.725 K.

In 1964, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson found an annoying hiss in a horn antenna at Bell Labs that they couldn’t get rid of, even after cleaning out pigeon droppings. It was this light. A small part of the static on an old analogue television was the Big Bang.

In 1990 the COBE satellite measured its spectrum and found the most perfect blackbody curve ever seen in nature, matching theory to better than 1 part in 10,000. That is strong evidence the early universe was hot, dense and in balance.

The math
λobs = (1 + z) λemit,  T ∝ 1/(1 + z),  λpeak = 2.898 mm·K / T

A blackbody stays a blackbody as it stretches, just cooler. The curve is drawn per unit wavelength and scaled to keep its peak height constant; in reality it dims as (1 + z)⁴. Peak per unit frequency today is at 160 GHz (1.9 mm).

03Reading the ripples

Sound waves frozen into the sky

First peak—
Shape of space—
Matches Planck?—

The glow is not perfectly even. Spots differ by about 1 part in 100,000, set by sound waves that rippled through the plasma. Dark matter clumps pulled the plasma in, the pressure of light pushed it back out, and it rang like a bell. When the fog cleared, the ripples were frozen in place.

The waves had only 380,000 years to travel, so there is a preferred spot size: about 1° across, twice the width of the full Moon. That size works as a cosmic ruler. In a flat universe it appears at 1°; in a curved one, the ruler would look bigger or smaller. It looks exactly 1°: space is flat to within a fraction of a percent.

The heights of the later peaks weigh the ingredients. More ordinary matter makes the odd-numbered peaks stand taller. Fitting all the peaks gives the universe’s recipe: about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter (Φ 024) and 68% dark energy (Φ 025).

A teaching model, not a full calculation: it captures where the peaks fall, the baryon loading R that lifts odd peaks, and damping at small scales, but real spectra come from codes like CAMB and CLASS solving the full physics. The sky patch is about 20° × 10°, built from random waves with this spectrum. The faint curve is the model at Planck’s best-fit values.

Observations

Four portraits of the baby universe

1964–65

Penzias and Wilson

Two radio engineers at Bell Labs found the background by accident; nearby, Robert Dicke’s team at Princeton was building an experiment to look for it. Nobel Prize, 1978.

1989–1993

COBE

Measured the perfect blackbody spectrum and, in 1992, the first ripples. George Smoot called it like seeing the face of God. Nobel Prize for John Mather and Smoot, 2006.

2001–2010

WMAP

Mapped the ripples across the whole sky and pinned down the age of the universe, 13.7 billion years, to about 1%.

2009–2013

Planck

The sharpest map yet, giving age 13.8 billion years and the 5% / 27% / 68% recipe, with a few puzzling anomalies, like a large cold spot, still under study.

Note to self

The microwave background is light from 380,000 years after the Big Bang; its frozen sound waves show that space is flat and that ordinary matter is only 5% of the universe.
