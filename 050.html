scalar waves

FRINGE SCIENCE · scalar and longitudinal electromagnetic waves — a hidden kind of wave, said to pass through matter, carry energy with little loss, and act where ordinary fields are zero

The phrase “scalar waves” covers several ideas: Nikola Tesla’s talk of waves through the Earth itself, Tom Bearden’s “scalar electromagnetics”, and Konstantin Meyl’s demonstrations of wireless energy transfer. Ordinary light and radio waves are transverse; the claim is that a second, overlooked side of electromagnetism exists. Its supporters point out that physics has been surprised before: the potentials once thought to be mere bookkeeping turned out to act on electrons directly, and Tesla’s ringing Earth turned out to be real. Here are those two vindications, and a modern demonstration.

01Aharonov–Bohm, 1959 · Tonomura, 1986

Acting where the field is zero

Magnetic field on the electrons’ paths—
Shift of the stripes—
Confirmed by—

Classical electromagnetism describes forces with fields, and treats the underlying potentials as a mathematical convenience. In 1959 Yakir Aharonov and David Bohm predicted that quantum particles would feel a potential even in a region where every field is zero.

Their test: send electrons around both sides of a shielded magnet. Outside the magnet there is no magnetic field at all, yet the interference pattern should shift as the magnet changes.

The case forIn 1986 Akira Tonomura’s team at Hitachi used electron holography and a superconducting shield to show the effect beyond doubt. Physics had to accept that potentials are physically real and act where fields vanish. Scalar-wave proponents argue that this opens exactly the door they describe: if potentials can act without fields, then waves in the potentials, invisible to ordinary field detectors, deserve a serious search.
The case againstThe Aharonov–Bohm shift is fixed by the magnetic flux enclosed by the paths, and a pure wave in the potentials with no field changes nothing measurable.

02Tesla, 1899 · Schumann, 1952

The Earth rings like a bell

Resonance shown—
Wavelength—
Predicted · measured—

At Colorado Springs in 1899 Tesla built a giant magnifying transmitter and studied lightning storms. He concluded that the Earth itself could be set into electrical resonance, standing waves circling the globe, and that this could carry signals and power to any point on its surface.

For decades the idea was treated as a fantasy of Tesla’s later years.

The case forIn 1952 the physicist Winfried Otto Schumann predicted that the space between the ground and the ionosphere forms a global cavity with natural frequencies around 8 hertz, and in 1960 Balser and Wagner measured them. The Earth really does ring, driven by lightning just as Tesla described. Supporters argue that Tesla grasped a global electrical resonance decades before mainstream physics, and that his plans to use it for power were abandoned for financial, not physical, reasons.
The case againstThe Schumann resonances are weak and lossy, and nothing in their physics suggests power could be transmitted through them efficiently, or that they involve a new kind of wave.

The math
fn ≈ (c / 2πR) √(n(n + 1)),  measured lower because of losses

For an ideal cavity the first mode would be at about 10.6 Hz; the real ionosphere is a lossy boundary, which lowers it to the measured 7.8 Hz. The displayed frequencies are the measured ones.

03Furtwangen, 2000s

Meyl’s transmitter

Received power, Meyl’s reported trend—
Ordinary near-field coupling—
LEDs at the receiver—

Konstantin Meyl, a professor of electrical engineering at Furtwangen University in Germany, built demonstration kits with a transmitter coil and a receiver coil, each a flat spiral connected to a ball electrode, tuned to the same frequency.

He reported that LEDs at the receiver light brightly while the transmitter seems to draw little power, that the coupling weakens with distance far more slowly than ordinary fields would, and that the signal arrives faster than light.

The case forMeyl interprets the effect as Tesla’s longitudinal waves, supported by an extended field theory he has published in books and papers, and his kits have been shown at conferences and used by students to repeat the demonstration. The sharp dependence on tuning matches his claim that the receiver resonates with a wave rather than picking up a stray field. If the slow fall-off is real, it would revive Tesla’s dream of wireless power.
The case againstIndependent analyses find ordinary resonant near-field coupling with a return path through ground or the experimenter’s body, and tests at larger distances show the usual steep fall-off.
Try thisMove the coils apart and compare the two curves. Then detune the receiver and watch the LEDs go out.
The math
near-field power ∝ 1/r6;  Meyl’s reported trend: much slower

The ordinary curve is the steep fall-off of magnetically coupled coils beyond their own size. The second curve is schematic, following the slower decline described in Meyl’s demonstrations, not a measured dataset.
