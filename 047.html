tired light

FRINGE SCIENCE · an alternative to cosmic expansion — the idea that light from distant galaxies is reddened because it loses energy on its long journey, not because space is stretching

In 1929 Edwin Hubble found that the farther a galaxy, the redder its light. The standard reading is that space is expanding. That same year the Caltech astronomer Fritz Zwicky, who later discovered the first evidence for dark matter, suggested another: light simply gets “tired”, losing a little energy for every million light-years it travels. Hubble himself remained cautious about expansion for years. Tired light has returned in new forms, and its supporters point to tests where a non-expanding universe fits the data more simply. Here are three of them.

01Tolman’s test, 1930 · 2014

How fast do distant galaxies dim?

Dimming in a static universe—
Measured (Lerner et al.)—

In 1930 the physicist Richard Tolman proposed a direct test of expansion: the surface brightness of galaxies, how bright each patch of a galaxy looks. In an expanding universe it should fall very steeply with redshift; in a static universe it should barely change, whatever causes the redshift.

The comparison needs galaxies of the same kind, measured in matching wavebands, near and far.

The case forIn 2014 Eric Lerner, Renato Falomo and Riccardo Scarpa compared the ultraviolet surface brightness of about a thousand disk galaxies from the nearby universe out to redshift five, in the International Journal of Modern Physics D. They found that surface brightness stayed essentially constant, as a static universe predicts, with no need for the strong evolution the expanding model requires to explain the result away.
The case againstLubin and Sandage (2001) found dimming close to the expanding prediction once the expected brightening of younger stellar populations was included.

02The angular-size test

Should distant galaxies look bigger?

Apparent size in a static universe—
Smallest apparent size, expanding—

A strange prediction of the expanding universe is that very distant objects should look larger again. Light from them left when the universe was small and they were close to us, so beyond about redshift 1.6 a galaxy of fixed size should appear to grow on the sky.

In a static universe there is no such turnaround: the farther the galaxy, the smaller it looks.

The case forObserved galaxies keep getting smaller on the sky out to the greatest distances, with no sign of the turnaround. To save the expanding model, astronomers must assume galaxies of the same brightness were several times smaller in the past, an assumption that tired-light supporters say was added to fit the data. The James Webb telescope has found that early galaxies look strikingly small and compact, as a static geometry would suggest.
The case againstGalaxies are expected to grow over time by mergers and star formation, and independent evidence supports that growth.

θ ∝ 1 / dA,  expanding: dA = dC / (1 + z);  static: d = (c/H₀) ln(1 + z)

03Ottawa, 2023

Time enough for early galaxies

Time to form it, standard model—
Time to form it, hybrid model—
Age of the universe—

The James Webb Space Telescope has found galaxies that look too massive and mature for the few hundred million years the standard model allows them (Φ 046).

In 2023 the physicist Rajendra Gupta of the University of Ottawa proposed a model combining tired light with slowly varying constants of nature, and fitted it to supernova and galaxy data.

The case forPublished in Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society, Gupta’s model fits the supernova distance data as well as the standard model and gives the universe an age of 26.7 billion years. Early galaxies then have billions of years, not hundreds of millions, to grow, which removes the tension with Webb’s observations. Tired light, in this picture, accounts for part of the redshift that is usually attributed to expansion.
The case againstThe model has not been shown to reproduce the microwave background and the stretched light curves of distant supernovae, which strongly support expansion.
