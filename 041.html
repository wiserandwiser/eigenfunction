cold fusion

FRINGE SCIENCE · low-energy nuclear reactions (LENR) — the claim that nuclear reactions can happen at room temperature, inside a metal packed with heavy hydrogen

In March 1989 two respected electrochemists, Martin Fleischmann and Stanley Pons, announced that a tabletop cell of palladium and heavy water was giving off more heat than any chemistry could explain. Many quick replications failed and the field was written off. But a community of scientists, at SRI International, the US Navy and laboratories in Japan, Italy and India, kept working, and found that the effect appears only under demanding conditions most early attempts never reached. In 2023 the US Department of Energy’s ARPA-E began funding the research again. Here you can see how a metal lattice helps nuclei meet, load a palladium cathode past the threshold, and check the heat against the helium.

01Screening

The lattice lowers the wall

Fusion rate boost from the lattice—
Screening predicted for a gas—
Screening measured in metals—

Deuterium nuclei repel each other. To fuse they must tunnel (Φ 002) through a wall of electrical repulsion about a million electronvolts high. Inside a metal, though, they are surrounded by a sea of electrons that partly cancels their charge, lowering the wall. This screening is mainstream physics.

What surprised physicists was how strong it turned out to be.

The case forAccelerator experiments in the 2000s, by Claus Rolfs’ group at Bochum and Konrad Czerski’s in Berlin and Szczecin, fired deuterons into deuterium-loaded metals and found screening energies of 300 eV and more, many times the 25 eV expected from a gas, boosting fusion rates at low energy by large factors. Proponents argue that a densely loaded lattice, with defects, vacancies and collective electron motion, could amplify this much further, and that the measured anomaly shows the metal environment matters in ways standard theory did not foresee.
The case againstEven the largest measured screening leaves rates at room temperature tens of orders of magnitude below what a watt of heat would need.
Try thisSet the screening to the gas value of 25 eV, then raise it to the 300 eV measured in metals, and watch the boost at low collision energies. Lower the collision energy: the slower the nuclei, the more screening matters.
The math
boost ≈ exp(π η Ue / E),  η = Sommerfeld parameter

This is the standard enhancement factor used in accelerator studies of screening, with E the collision energy in the centre-of-mass frame and Ue the screening energy. For deuterium, 2πη ≈ 31.3 / √E(keV).

02SRI International, 1990s

The loading threshold

Excess power—
Calorimeter error—
Above the threshold?—

The original evidence was heat. Run current through heavy water with a palladium cathode, and deuterium is driven into the metal. Measure the power going in and the heat coming out; if more comes out, something is adding energy.

Michael McKubre’s team at SRI International, funded by the Electric Power Research Institute, ran hundreds of carefully instrumented cells through the 1990s and found the key that explained the early failures: excess heat appeared only when the palladium was loaded to about 0.9 deuterium atoms per palladium atom or more, a level hard to reach and to keep.

The case forSRI’s mass-flow calorimeters measured excess power many times above their errors, with a clear dependence on loading and current density, the hallmark of a real physical effect rather than noise. Most 1989 replications never reached the required loading, which explains why they saw nothing. Excess heat has since been reported by groups at the US Navy, ENEA in Italy and in Japan, sometimes in bursts lasting days, with total energies far beyond any chemical store in the cell.
The case againstResults remain hard to repeat on demand, and a Google-funded effort reported in Nature in 2019 found no excess heat, though it also struggled to reach high loading.
Try thisStart at a loading of 0.80 and watch a flat record. Raise it past 0.90 and run a few cells: bursts of excess power appear, larger than the calorimeter’s error band.
The math
excess power = Pout − Pin,  appears above x = D/Pd ≈ 0.88–0.90

The simulated cell follows the pattern McKubre reported: no excess below the threshold, then excess that grows with loading above it and comes in irregular bursts. The calorimeter error band is about ±1% of input power.

03China Lake, 1991–

Heat and helium go together

Energy per helium atom—
Predicted for D + D → ⁴He—
Helium in control cells—

A nuclear reaction leaves ash. If two deuterium nuclei combine into helium-4, each reaction releases 23.8 million electronvolts. So if the excess heat is nuclear and helium is the product, the amount of helium should be proportional to the heat, at a known ratio.

That is a hard prediction to fake: chemistry makes no helium, and helium leaking in from the air has no reason to track the heat a cell produces.

The case forMelvin Miles at the Naval Air Warfare Center at China Lake collected gas from heat-producing cells and found helium-4 in proportion to the excess energy, close to the 23.8 MeV per atom expected, while cells without excess heat showed none. McKubre’s group at SRI and later analyses reported the same ratio. A correlation between two independently measured quantities, at the ratio nuclear physics predicts, is the strongest evidence the heat is nuclear.
The case againstHelium is present in air at 5 parts per million and the amounts are close to detection limits, and no known mechanism lets a nucleus release 23.8 MeV into a lattice without a gamma ray.
Try thisCompare all cells with the controls only. Read the fitted energy per helium atom and compare it with 23.8 MeV.
The math
1 W for 1 s → 1 / (23.8 MeV) ≈ 2.6 × 1011 helium atoms

Each simulated heat-producing cell yields helium at the D + D → ⁴He ratio with measurement scatter; control cells show only a small background. The fitted slope gives the energy per atom.

Milestones

1989

The announcement

Fleischmann and Pons reported excess heat from palladium in heavy water at a press conference in Utah.

1990s

The threshold

SRI International’s work, funded by the electric power industry, linked excess heat to deuterium loading above about 0.9.

2004

A second look

A US Department of Energy review split roughly evenly on whether the excess-heat evidence was convincing.

2023

ARPA-E returns

The Department of Energy’s advanced research agency announced funding for new low-energy nuclear reaction projects.

Note to self

Cold fusion’s supporters point to heat that appears above a loading threshold and helium that tracks it; after three decades, the US government is funding the question again.
