QUANTUM · interference — two paths adding up, so that some places get more and some get none

Fire single particles, one at a time, at a wall with two slits. Each one lands as a single dot. Yet together the dots draw a pattern only a wave could make, and the pattern vanishes the moment you look to see which slit each particle used.

Start with something familiar: water waves. A wave rolls up to a wall with two narrow openings. Each opening becomes a new source of ripples, and the two sets spread out and overlap.

Where a crest from one slit meets a crest from the other, they add up into a bigger wave. Where a crest meets a trough, they cancel to nothing. On the screen at the right this makes stripes of bright and dark. This is interference, the signature of waves. Thomas Young used it around 1801 to show that light is a wave.

Cover one slit and the stripes disappear, leaving one broad glow. The dark bands only exist because there are two ways to get there, and they cancel.

Try thisShorten the wavelength or widen the gap and the bands crowd together. Then cover one slit, and notice that some dark spots get brighter. Opening a second path made those places darker.
The math
I(θ) ∝ cos²(πd sin θλ) · sinc²(πa sin θλ)

Bright bands sit where the path difference d sin θ is a whole number of wavelengths. Their spacing on a distant screen is about λL/d. The sinc² factor is the broad glow from each slit’s own width a. The ripple tank sums circular waves from points across each slit, with amplitude falling as 1/√r.

Now turn the source down until only one particle is in flight at a time. Each one arrives at the screen as a single dot, in one place, never a smeared wave. Where it lands looks completely random.

Keep going. After a few dozen, nothing. After a few hundred, stripes begin to show. After thousands, the dots have drawn exactly the bright and dark bands of Bay 01.

So each particle on its own somehow “knew” about both slits. It travelled as a wave of possibility through both openings, the wave interfered with itself, and only on arrival did it land as one dot, with the odds set by the wave.

This isn’t a thought experiment. In 1989 a team at Hitachi filmed electrons building up this pattern one by one. Physicists have since done it with buckyballs (60 carbon atoms, 1999) and with molecules of about 2,000 atoms (2019).

Try thisClear the screen, slow the rate right down, and try to predict where the next dot will land. Then speed up and watch the bands appear from the noise.
The math
P(x) = |ψ₁(x) + ψ₂(x)|²

The Born rule: the chance of landing at x is the squared size of the total wave there. Add the waves first, then square. The cross term 2 Re(ψ₁*ψ₂) is the interference. Each dot here is drawn at random from exactly this distribution. The wavelength is de Broglie’s λ = h/p, which is why heavy molecules need very slow speeds and very fine gratings.

The obvious next step is to catch each particle in the act: place a detector at the slits that records which one it went through.

The moment the detector can tell, the stripes disappear. The dots pile up in two plain humps, one behind each slit, as if they were ordinary bullets. No interference.

It isn’t that the detector kicks the particle off course. What matters is information. If the path is knowable even in principle, the two possibilities no longer overlap and can’t interfere. Make the detector only partly reliable and you get faint stripes. The trade-off is exact: the more you know about the path, the less contrast in the stripes.

Stranger still, if the which-path record is erased before anyone reads it, the stripes can be recovered among the matching events. This is the quantum eraser, first demonstrated around 2000.

Try thisSlide the detector from off to on, clearing the screen at each step. Watch the stripes fade in proportion. Check that the two readouts, squared, always add up to 1.
The math
V² + D² ≤ 1

Englert’s duality relation (1996). D is the distinguishability of the paths, and V the visibility of the fringes: (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin). With a perfect detector D = 1 and V = 0. The model uses P = ½(P₁ + P₂) + V√(P₁P₂) cos φ, with V = √(1 − D²).

Observations

Where interference is put to work

Measurement

Interferometers

Split a beam, send the halves down two paths, and recombine them. The stripes shift if either path changes by a fraction of a wavelength. LIGO’s gravitational-wave detector is this idea, 4 kilometres long.

Atoms

Matter-wave sensors

Atom interferometers split clouds of cold atoms the same way. They measure gravity and rotation precisely enough to map rock density underground and to navigate without GPS.

Microscopy

Electron microscopes

Electrons have far shorter wavelengths than light, so they resolve far finer detail. Cryo-electron microscopy, which won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, images proteins atom by atom.

Open question

How big can it go?

Researchers keep pushing interference to heavier objects to test whether quantum mechanics ever gives way. So far it hasn’t, up to molecules of tens of thousands of atomic mass units.

Note to self

A particle travels like a wave of possibilities through every open path, and lands like a dot. Learn which path it took, and the possibilities no longer interfere.
