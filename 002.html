tunnelling

QUANTUM · tunnelling — arriving on the far side of a wall you never had the energy to climb

Roll a ball at a hill too high for it and it rolls back, every time. A quantum particle sometimes turns up on the other side without ever going over the top. That leak keeps the Sun burning, sets the half-lives of radioactive elements, and lets a microscope map single atoms.

A quantum particle travels as a wave of probability: a packet of ripples whose height tells you how likely you are to find the particle there.

Here it runs at a wall taller than its energy. Most of the wave bounces back. But a small piece appears on the far side and keeps going, though the particle never had the energy to go over the top. That leak is tunnelling.

How it gets through

  1. The wave reaches into the wall.A ball has a hard edge: it is either touching the wall or it isn’t. A wave has no hard edge, so when it meets the wall it doesn’t stop dead at the surface. It seeps in.
  2. Inside, it fades but never stops.Within the wall the wave can’t ripple forward. Instead it shrinks steadily with depth, weaker and weaker, but it never quite reaches zero. Section 02 zooms in on this fade.
  3. A thin wall lets some survive.Whatever is left at the far face starts rippling again as an ordinary travelling wave, only much weaker. That weak wave is the pink part. Its size sets the chance of finding the particle over there.

The white dot is an ordinary ball with the same energy, rolling on the same hill. Below 100% it climbs part of the way up the slope, stops and rolls back. Above 100% it slows as it climbs, creeps over the top, and rolls down the far side. Either way it goes up and over or back, never through. It has a hard edge, so it never reaches into the wall. The ball never gets through. Only the quantum particle does, because only the particle is a wave.

Light does the same thing. Shine a beam into a glass prism at an angle where it reflects completely off the inside face. Then bring a second prism within a fraction of a wavelength of that face. Some light jumps the gap, even though by the ray picture it was trapped. It is the same fading wave, crossing a gap of air instead of a wall.

Nothing drills a hole. If you check where the particle is, you find it whole, on one side or the other. The wave only sets the odds, and for the far side those odds are small but never zero.

Zoom in on the wall. Now the particle is a real electron with 1 eV of energy, and the wall stands a little higher than that. You set how much higher with the shortfall slider.

Outside, the wave ripples. Inside the wall it stops rippling and simply fades, shrinking by nearly two-thirds every decay length. Whatever is left at the far face carries on as a much weaker wave, shown magnified so you can see it at all.

Because the fade is exponential, thickness matters enormously. Every extra decay length cuts the odds by about 7.4 times. Watch the chart below the wave: on a log scale, the odds fall along a straight line.

Mass matters too. A proton is 1,836 times heavier than an electron, so its decay length is about 43 times shorter. Through the same wall its odds drop off the chart almost at once.

And you? Treat yourself, generously, as a single 70 kg particle walking into a 10 cm wall, one joule short of the energy needed. Your odds come out around 1 in 10 to the power 10³⁴. A wall is safe.

The math
ψ(x) ∝ e−κx,  κ = √(2m(V₀ − E))ħ

Inside the wall the Schrödinger equation has decaying exponentials as solutions instead of waves. The decay length is 1/κ. Matching the wave and its slope at both faces gives the exact transmission:

T = [1 + V₀² sinh²(κa)4E(V₀ − E)]−1 ≈ 16 EV₀(1 − EV₀) e−2κa

The electron curve uses the exact form. The proton curve uses the approximation, which holds whenever κa ≫ 1.

Why the Sun shines at all

The core of the Sun is about 15 million degrees. That sounds hot, but a typical proton there carries only around 1 keV of energy.

To fuse, two protons must get close enough for the strong nuclear force to grab them. Their electric repulsion stands in the way: a hill around 1 MeV high, hundreds of times higher than they can climb. Classically you would need a core at around ten billion degrees. Every proton would turn back, and the Sun would be dark.

Tunnelling changes this. In the model, protons roll toward the hill, slow down and turn back. Now and then one passes through the slope into the well at the centre and fuses.

Each proton that is going to tunnel turns pink as soon as it appears, so you can follow it all the way in. Nature gives no such warning. Every proton at a given energy has the same odds, and which one gets through is pure chance.

Two effects fight here. Faster protons tunnel far more easily, but there are very few of them. Multiply the two and you get a narrow window of energies where nearly all fusion happens, called the Gamow peak. That narrow window is why the Sun burns slowly and steadily for about 10 billion years instead of exploding.

The math
Ptunnel(E) ≈ e−√(EG/E),  N(E) ∝ e−E/kT

The Gamow energy EG = 2mrc²(παZ₁Z₂)² is about 493 keV for two protons. Maximising the product gives the peak:

E₀ = (EG(kT)² / 4)1/3

The rate readout integrates (kT)−3/2 ∫ e−E/kT − √(EG/E) dE. Its slope gives the sensitivity: about T⁴ near the Sun’s core. The model ignores one more obstacle. The first step of the real proton–proton chain also needs the weak force to turn a proton into a neutron, so a given proton in the core waits billions of years on average.

Observations

Where tunnelling is doing the work

Radioactivity

Alpha decay

In 1928 George Gamow explained alpha decay as tunnelling. An alpha particle rattles inside the nucleus and leaks out through the same kind of electric hill. Small changes in the wall make huge changes in the odds, which is why half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years.

Microscopy

Seeing single atoms

The scanning tunnelling microscope (Binnig and Rohrer, Nobel 1986) holds a sharp tip a few ångströms above a surface. The tunnelling current changes about tenfold for every ångström of gap, so it traces the bumps of individual atoms.

Your pocket

Flash memory

A flash memory cell stores a bit as charge on a gate isolated by a thin layer of oxide. Writing and erasing push electrons through that insulating layer by tunnelling.

Quantum computers

Tunnelling you can wire up

The 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Clarke, Devoret and Martinis for showing macroscopic tunnelling in an electric circuit. The same kind of junction, where pairs of electrons tunnel through a thin barrier, is the heart of today’s superconducting qubits.

Note to self

A wave never stops dead at a wall. It fades inside, and if the wall is thin enough, some of it is still there on the far side.
