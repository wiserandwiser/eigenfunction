anti-gravity

FRINGE SCIENCE · anti-gravity devices — machines claimed to reduce, shield or modify gravity: spinning gyroscopes, superconducting discs and rotating rings

Gravity is the one force we can’t switch off or block. Yet three times in the last forty years, scientists working in respected laboratories have reported devices that seem to change it, and published in physics journals: a gyroscope that weighed less when spun one way, a superconducting disc that lightened whatever sat above it, and a rotating ring that produced a gravity-like field trillions of times stronger than Einstein’s theory allows. Each drew serious attention, from aerospace companies to the European Space Agency. Here are the three results.

01Tohoku, 1989

A gyroscope that weighed less

Published in—

In 1989 Hideo Hayasaka and Sakae Takeuchi, engineers at Tohoku University, sealed gyroscopes in containers and weighed them on a precision balance while they spun down from high speed.

They found an asymmetry no theory predicted: spinning clockwise as seen from above, the gyroscopes weighed slightly less, by an amount growing with the spin rate; spinning the other way, nothing changed.

The case forThe result was published in Physical Review Letters, among the most selective journals in physics, after peer review. The direction-dependence argues against ordinary artefacts such as air currents or heating, which would not care about the sense of rotation, and the signal grew smoothly with spin rate. A parity-violating link between rotation and gravity would be a major discovery, and some theories beyond general relativity allow it.
The case againstWithin a year three independent groups repeated the experiment with sealed rotors and better balances and found no change at sensitivities well below the claim.

02Tampere, 1992

A disc that lightened the air above it

Reported weight loss above the disc—
Sample—
Effect below the disc—

In 1992 the materials scientist Eugene Podkletnov, working at Tampere University of Technology in Finland, described a disc of yttrium barium copper oxide, a high-temperature superconductor, levitated in a magnetic field and set spinning.

Samples hung above the disc, he reported, lost a small fraction of their weight. The effect needed the disc to be superconducting and grew when it spun faster.

The case forPodkletnov published in Physica C, a peer-reviewed superconductivity journal, reporting weight losses of up to 0.3%, and later up to 2%, independent of the sample’s material. The effect appeared only with the disc superconducting, ruling out ordinary magnetism or air flow from a warm rotor. NASA’s Marshall Space Flight Center began building a replication, and Boeing was reported in 2002 to be funding work on it through a project called GRASP.
The case againstNo replication has reproduced the conditions and the effect together, and Podkletnov’s exact setup was never fully specified.
Try thisSpin the disc faster with it superconducting, then warm it up and watch the effect vanish.

03Seibersdorf, 2006

A ring that bent gravity

Ratio—

A spinning mass drags space-time around with it slightly, a gravitomagnetic effect predicted by Einstein and measured by the Gravity Probe B satellite. For laboratory masses it should be unmeasurably small.

Martin Tajmar and Clovis de Matos, at the Austrian Research Centers in Seibersdorf, with funding from the European Space Agency, cooled a niobium ring until it superconducted and then accelerated it rapidly while sensitive accelerometers watched nearby.

The case forIn 2006 the team reported a gravity-like acceleration in the sensors whenever the superconducting ring was spun up, around a ten-thousandth of Earth’s gravity, and announced through ESA that it was some 100 million trillion times larger than general relativity predicts. It appeared only below the superconducting temperature, linking gravity and quantum materials in a way standard physics does not allow. The group repeated the signal over many runs.
The case againstA ring-laser test at the University of Canterbury in New Zealand found no effect, and Tajmar’s later, improved experiments traced much of the signal to mechanical and sensor effects.
