FRINGE SCIENCE · the Electric Universe — the claim that electric currents in plasma, not gravity alone, shape the cosmos and help power the stars

More than 99% of the visible universe is plasma, gas so hot that its electrons are free, and plasma carries electric currents. The Electric Universe, promoted since the 1990s by Wallace Thornhill, David Talbott and the Thunderbolts Project, argues that astronomy has been slow to take this seriously. History offers support: currents in space were once dismissed and later measured, and the Sun’s outer atmosphere is still hundreds of times hotter than its surface for reasons that are debated. Here you can switch on the currents that light the aurora, climb through the Sun’s atmosphere, and watch currents twist plasma into a spiral.

01Birkeland, 1908 · confirmed 1966

Currents that light the sky

Current into the auroral zones—
Proposed—
Confirmed by satellite—

In 1908 the Norwegian physicist Kristian Birkeland argued, from polar expeditions and laboratory experiments with a magnetised sphere in a vacuum chamber, that the aurora is powered by electric currents flowing from space along Earth’s magnetic field lines.

For half a century most British and American physicists rejected the idea, holding that space was an insulating vacuum that could not carry such currents.

The case forIn 1966 magnetometers on a US Navy satellite detected the field-aligned currents Birkeland had predicted, now called Birkeland currents and known to carry millions of amperes. The episode is the Electric Universe’s model case: electrical effects in space were dismissed on theoretical grounds and then measured. Proponents argue that the same currents, scaled up, run through the solar system and the galaxy, and that the field has still not fully absorbed the lesson.
The case againstMainstream space physics fully accepts Birkeland currents today; the disagreement is about whether currents on larger scales do what the Electric Universe claims.

02The corona

A fire is hottest at its centre and cooler as you move away. The Sun’s visible surface, at about 5,800 K, is far cooler than its core, as expected. But just above the surface the temperature falls only to about 4,000 K and then climbs, reaching more than a million kelvin in the corona.

The physicist Bengt Edlén showed in the 1940s that strange green lines in the corona came from iron atoms stripped of thirteen electrons, which needs temperatures of a million degrees or more.

The case forHeating from inside cannot make the outer layer hotter than the surface beneath it; something must deliver energy from outside or within the corona itself. The engineer Ralph Juergens proposed in 1972 that the Sun behaves like the anode of a giant electrical discharge, with currents heating its upper atmosphere just as discharges heat gas in the laboratory. After eighty years, the coronal heating problem is still listed as one of the major open questions in solar physics.
The case againstSolar physicists attribute the heating to magnetic energy released in waves and small flares, and probes such as Parker Solar Probe have measured some of these processes; solar neutrinos also show fusion powers the core.

03Peratt, 1986

Currents that build a spiral

Pull between the filaments—
Shape forming—
Gravity used—

Parallel electric currents attract each other. In plasma, currents tend to bunch into filaments, which are seen everywhere from lightning and laboratory pinches to the threads of the solar corona and long filaments in interstellar space.

Anthony Peratt of Los Alamos National Laboratory, a collaborator of the Nobel laureate Hannes Alfvén, ran large computer simulations of two such filaments interacting, using only electromagnetic forces.

The case forPeratt’s simulations, published in IEEE Transactions on Plasma Science in 1986, produced shapes strikingly like galaxies: double radio sources, then spiral arms trailing from a rotating core, with flat rotation curves arising naturally from the electromagnetic forces. Plasma behaves the same way over enormous ranges of scale, so Electric Universe proponents argue that what a laboratory pinch does in a nanosecond, a galactic current can do over millions of years, without invoking unseen dark matter.
The case againstLarge-scale magnetic fields in galaxies are measured to be weak, far below what would be needed for electromagnetism to outweigh gravity on these scales.
