precognition

FRINGE SCIENCE · knowing the future — the claim that information about events can reach us before they happen

Premonitions are among the most widely reported unusual experiences, from dream diaries to warnings before disasters. Since the 1990s the claim has moved into the laboratory, where it can be tested with computers that choose the future at random only after a person has responded. In 2011 one of social psychology’s most respected researchers published nine such experiments in a top journal, eight of them significant. Here you can rerun his curtain test, watch the body react before a picture appears, and pool ninety experiments from labs around the world.

01Feeling the future, 2011

Behind which curtain?

In Daryl Bem’s first experiment, 100 Cornell students saw two curtains on a computer screen and chose the one they felt hid a picture. Only after each choice did a random number generator decide where the picture actually was, so no clue could exist at the moment of choosing.

When the hidden picture was erotic, students chose correctly 53.1% of the time, where 50% is chance. For neutral pictures they scored 49.8%. The future picture seemed to pull the choice, but only when it mattered emotionally.

The case forBem, an emeritus professor at Cornell, ran nine experiments with more than 1,000 participants using standard, well-understood methods; eight were significant. The paper passed peer review at the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, one of the field’s leading journals. The target was chosen by a random generator after the response, which rules out ordinary cues entirely, and the effect appeared only for emotional targets, as an evolved ability to anticipate danger or opportunity would predict.
The case againstDirect replications by Ritchie, Wiseman and French and by Galak and colleagues in 2012 found effects close to zero, and the pre-registered Transparent Psi Project (2023) found 49.9%.

02Presentiment

The body reacts first

In presentiment experiments, pioneered by Dean Radin and Dick Bierman in the 1990s, a computer shows a series of calm and emotional pictures while measuring heart rate, pupil size or skin conductance. Each picture is chosen at random at the moment it appears.

Averaged over many trials, the body begins to react a few seconds before emotional pictures, before the computer has even chosen them. If the result is real, the nervous system is responding to something that has not happened yet.

The case forIn 2012 Julia Mossbridge, Patrizio Tressoldi and Jessica Utts published a meta-analysis of 26 studies from seven labs in Frontiers in Psychology. They found a small but highly significant pre-stimulus effect, with odds against chance of more than a hundred billion to one, and argued that expectation effects could not account for it because the pictures were chosen at random each time.
The case againstCritics such as Dalkvist and Wackermann show that anticipation, sequence construction and the way baselines are set can produce a similar pattern; some designs that remove these show smaller effects.
Try thisSet the effect to Mossbridge’s 0.21 and look at the gap that opens before zero seconds. Rerun with new volunteers: the gap persists, though single volunteers are noisy.
The math
pre-stimulus effect size = (meanemotional − meancalm) / σ

Each simulated volunteer sees 40 pictures, a quarter of them emotional and each chosen at random when shown. The effect you set adds an anticipatory rise before emotional pictures only, with an effect size of 0.21 matching the 2012 meta-analysis.

03Ninety experiments, 2015

The pooled evidence

One paper proves little; the real test is what happens when other laboratories try. In 2015 Bem, Patrizio Tressoldi, Thomas Rabeyron and Michael Duggan gathered every experiment they could find that tested the 2011 effects: 90 experiments from 33 laboratories in 14 countries, with more than 12,000 participants.

Individually many results were small or not significant. Together they pointed the same way, and the effect remained when Bem’s own experiments were removed.

The case forThe meta-analysis, published in F1000Research, reported a combined result with odds against chance of about ten billion to one and a Bayes factor in the billions, decisive evidence by conventional standards. Fast-thinking tasks, where people respond within a second, showed the strongest effects. The authors also estimated that hundreds of unpublished null studies would be needed to cancel the result.
The case againstCritics argue that flexible analysis in the pooled studies inflates the result, and large pre-registered replications designed to settle the question, such as the Transparent Psi Project, found nothing.

Milestones

1927

Dunne’s diaries

Aeronautical engineer J. W. Dunne’s An Experiment with Time urged readers to keep dream diaries to catch glimpses of the future.

1967

The Premonitions Bureau

After the Aberfan disaster, psychiatrist John Barker collected premonitions written down before events, including several of the disaster itself.

2011

Feeling the Future

Daryl Bem published nine experiments in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, eight of them significant.

2015

Ninety experiments

Bem and colleagues pooled 90 experiments from 33 labs in 14 countries and reported a highly significant overall effect.

Note to self

With the future chosen at random only after people respond, results keep landing above chance; for its supporters, precognition is a small, real effect that ordinary physics has yet to explain.
