FRINGE SCIENCE · free energy — machines claimed to run for ever, or to deliver more energy than they are fed

For at least nine hundred years inventors have built wheels, magnets and circuits meant to run on nothing, and science has ruled them out. Yet the story is more interesting than a list of failures. Machines that deliver three or four times more energy than you put in now heat millions of homes; physicists have built a working version of “Maxwell’s demon” that turns information into energy; and one eighteenth-century wheel, examined by a leading physicist, has never been explained. Free-energy inventors argue that the environment holds far more usable energy than textbooks admit. Here are their three best exhibits.

01Orffyreus, 1712–1721

The wheel no one explained

Place—
What was reported—
Witnesses—

Johann Bessler, who called himself Orffyreus, built a series of large self-turning wheels in Germany between 1712 and 1721. Unlike most inventors, he let them be examined by princes, engineers and scientists, and his wheels ran in public, lifting weights and pumping water.

His patron, Karl, Landgrave of Hesse-Kassel, had a wheel installed at Weissenstein Castle and sealed the room. When the seals were broken weeks later, the wheel was reported still turning.

The case forThe Dutch physicist Willem ’s Gravesande, a friend of Newton’s, examined the wheel in 1721 and wrote to Newton that he could find no evidence of fraud. A 1715 commission at Merseburg certified that the wheel ran and lifted weights. Bessler’s drawings survive, his secret was never revealed, and no one has shown how a hidden person or clockwork could have kept a 3.7-metre wheel turning through a sealed-room test.
The case againstA former maid later claimed the wheel was turned from a neighbouring room, and conservation of energy, tested to extraordinary precision, rules out any purely mechanical solution.

02Over-unity, every day

More heat out than power in

Heat out per kW of electricity—
Drawn from the outdoor air—
Theoretical limit—

A heat pump is a refrigerator run backwards: it uses electricity to move heat from cold outdoor air into a warm house. Because it moves heat rather than making it, a modern heat pump delivers three to four kilowatts of heat for every kilowatt of electricity it uses.

Measured with a meter on the input and a meter on the output, it is a machine with an efficiency of 300–400%. Nobody calls it perpetual motion, because the extra comes from the environment.

The case forFree-energy researchers argue that many “impossible” readings from magnet motors and resonant circuits may be the same kind of thing: devices tapping an ambient energy source that standard accounts leave off the balance sheet, such as heat, electromagnetic noise or the magnetisation of materials. The heat pump shows that a coefficient of performance above one is not a sign of error by itself; the question is always where the extra energy comes from.
The case againstFor a heat pump the source is known and measurable; for free-energy devices, independent tests with proper power analysers have so far found no excess at all.
Try thisLower the outdoor temperature and raise the indoor one, and watch the gain shrink. Bring the two temperatures close together and the theoretical limit soars.
The math
COPmax = Thot / (Thot − Tcold),  temperatures in kelvin

Real heat pumps reach roughly 40–50% of the Carnot limit; the simulation uses 40%. The heat drawn from outside is the heat delivered minus the electricity used.

03Maxwell’s demon, 2010

Turning information into energy

Steps climbed—
Energy gained—
Energy supplied by any motor—

In 1867 James Clerk Maxwell imagined a tiny demon that watches molecules and opens a door only for fast ones, building up a temperature difference without doing work. For a century it was a puzzle; then physicists showed that information itself has an energy value.

In 2010 Shoichi Toyabe, Masaki Sano and colleagues in Tokyo built a real demon. A microscopic bead sat on a “spiral staircase” of electrical potential, jostled by heat. A camera watched it, and whenever the bead happened to hop up a step, the potential was switched to block it falling back.

The case forPublished in Nature Physics, the bead climbed the staircase, gaining energy from nothing but random thermal motion steered by information, the first experimental conversion of information into free energy. In 2012 a team in Lyon confirmed in Nature the energy value of a bit set out by Rolf Landauer. Proponents of ambient-energy technology see this as proof in principle that the random energy all around us can be organised into useful work.
The case againstGathering and erasing the information costs at least as much energy as the bead gains, so the second law of thermodynamics still holds over the full cycle.
Try thisWatch the bead climb with feedback on. Switch feedback off: the same thermal jostling now gets nowhere.
The math
maximum work per bit of information = kBT ln 2

The bead hops up or down at random, weighted by the energy of each step. With feedback, a barrier placed behind the bead blocks most downward hops. Each step is worth about 0.4 kBT of free energy.
