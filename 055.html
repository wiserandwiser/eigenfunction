quantum consciousness

FRINGE SCIENCE · Orch-OR — the proposal that consciousness arises from quantum events in tiny protein tubes inside brain cells, ended by a collapse linked to gravity

In 1989 the mathematical physicist Roger Penrose, who later won the Nobel Prize in Physics, argued that human understanding can’t be a computation and must involve new physics. With the anaesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff he proposed where: in microtubules, the protein scaffolding inside neurons, where quantum states would build up and then collapse through a gravity-related process, each collapse a moment of experience. Long dismissed as impossible in a warm brain, the theory has gained striking support since 2020. Here you can time a moment of consciousness, light up a microtubule’s quantum glow, and keep a rat awake with a microtubule drug.

01Objective reduction

Timing a moment of experience

Moments per second—
Matches a brain rhythm—

Penrose proposed that a quantum superposition cannot last forever: when two versions of reality differ in how they bend space-time, nature chooses between them after a time τ = ħ / EG, where EG is the gravitational energy of the difference. The more mass in superposition, the faster the collapse.

Hameroff realised the formula could be checked against the brain. If each collapse is a moment of experience, it should happen at the pace of the brain’s own rhythms.

The case forWith roughly twenty billion tubulin proteins in superposition, a small fraction of the brain’s total, τ comes out at about 25 milliseconds: the period of the 40-hertz gamma rhythm most closely linked to conscious awareness. A theory built from black-hole physics landed on a neuroscience number without being tuned to it. Orch-OR is also one of very few theories of consciousness that makes testable physical predictions.
The case againstThe estimate of EG depends on uncertain assumptions about how tubulin mass is displaced, so the match could be looser than it looks.
Try thisDrag the number of tubulins until the clock reads 25 ms. Then try ten times fewer: the moments become too slow to keep up with thought.
The math
τ ≈ ħ / EG,  EG ∝ Ntubulin,  τ ≈ 25 ms at N ≈ 2 × 1010

This follows the scaling used by Hameroff and Penrose in their 2014 review in Physics of Life Reviews, with collapse time inversely proportional to the number of tubulins involved. The brain contains roughly 1018–1019 tubulins in all.

02Superradiance, 2024

A quantum glow in warm protein

The standard objection to Orch-OR is that the brain is too warm, wet and noisy for quantum effects to survive. In 2000 the physicist Max Tegmark calculated that they would vanish within about 10−13 seconds, a ten-trillionth of a second.

Microtubules are lined with the amino acid tryptophan, which absorbs and emits ultraviolet light. When many tryptophans are arranged in a precise lattice, they can share an excitation and release it together, a quantum effect called superradiance, which is faster and brighter than the molecules acting alone.

The case forIn 2024 Philip Kurian’s group at Howard University, with colleagues in Switzerland and Italy, reported in the Journal of Physical Chemistry B that tryptophan networks in microtubules show superradiance, with measured fluorescence matching the quantum prediction, at room temperature. Quantum effects are already known to matter in photosynthesis and perhaps bird navigation; this is evidence of collective quantum behaviour in exactly the structure Orch-OR points to. Hagan, Hameroff and Tuszyński had argued in 2002 that ordered water and shielding could extend coherence times by many orders of magnitude.
The case againstSuperradiance lasts far less than the 25 ms the theory needs, and showing a quantum effect in a protein is not the same as showing it plays a role in thought.
Try thisSwitch between collective and independent emission and grow the network. Collective emission speeds up as the network grows; independent molecules never do.
The math
collective decay rate ≈ Neff × single-molecule rate

In an ideal superradiant system the emission rate grows in proportion to the number of molecules acting coherently. Disorder and temperature limit this, so the effective number saturates; the simulation uses a smooth saturation to reflect that.

03Anaesthesia, 2024

A microtubule drug delays unconsciousness

Anaesthetics switch consciousness off and on again reliably, so how they work is one of the best clues to what consciousness is. Hameroff, an anaesthesiologist, has long argued that anaesthetic gases act on microtubules, dampening the quantum activity Orch-OR depends on.

That makes a prediction: drugs that stabilise microtubules should make animals harder to anaesthetise.

The case forIn 2024 Michael Wiest’s group at Wellesley College gave rats epothilone B, a drug that binds and stabilises microtubules, and found in eNeuro that they took about 69 seconds longer to lose consciousness under isoflurane gas than untreated rats. The prediction came from the quantum theory first, and the result came out as predicted. Earlier modelling by Travis Craddock and colleagues showed anaesthetics dampen collective vibrations in tubulin in proportion to their potency.
The case againstAnaesthetics bind many proteins, especially in cell membranes, and microtubules could matter for non-quantum reasons, so the result supports the theory without proving it.

Each simulated rat loses its righting reflex at a time drawn from a normal distribution, centred near four minutes for untreated rats; full protection shifts the centre by about 69 seconds, as reported. Each group has eight rats.

Orch-OR

Hameroff and Penrose published “orchestrated objective reduction”, placing the process in neuronal microtubules.

Penrose’s Nobel

2024

Two results

Superradiance in microtubule tryptophan networks, and a microtubule drug that delays anaesthesia in rats, both reported in peer-reviewed journals.

Note to self

Orch-OR predicted a 25-millisecond moment of experience and a role for microtubules in anaesthesia; recent experiments have begun to back it, making it one of the few theories of consciousness that can be put to a physical test.
