light sails

BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · interstellar travel — riding a laser beam to another star, by pushing with light itself

Light has no mass, but it carries momentum: every photon that bounces off a mirror gives it a tiny push. Make the mirror light enough and the light strong enough, and the push becomes enormous. Breakthrough Starshot’s plan: a ground-based laser array pushes gram-scale probes to a fifth of the speed of light in minutes, reaching Alpha Centauri in about twenty years.

01Pushing with light

Photon pressure

A photon of energy E carries momentum E/c. When it reflects off a mirror it reverses, handing the mirror twice its momentum. Sunlight near Earth pushes on a perfect mirror with about 9 micronewtons per square metre, roughly the weight of a few grains of sand on a whole square metre.

That is enough to sail through the solar system slowly. Japan’s IKAROS (2010) was the first craft driven by sunlight, and the Planetary Society’s LightSail 2 (2019) raised its orbit around Earth with a 32 m² sail.

To reach another star within a human lifetime you need far more. Point a 100-gigawatt laser at a sail plus probe weighing about one gram and the push is hundreds of newtons, an acceleration of tens of thousands of g. No rocket can come close, because the craft carries no fuel at all: the engine stays on the ground.

The math
F = 2P/c  (perfect reflector),  a = F/m

For a perfect mirror facing the light. Sunlight delivers about 1,360 W/m² near Earth. 1 g = 9.81 m/s². Real sails reflect around 90% and absorb a little, which also heats them; at 100 GW, absorbing even a millionth of the light deposits 100 kW into a gram of material, so sail materials must be almost perfectly reflective.

The catch is that a laser beam spreads. Even a perfect beam diverges by an angle of about the wavelength divided by the width of the emitter. As the sail races away, the spot of light grows until it is larger than the sail and most of the light misses. The sail can only be pushed while it is close.

So the plan uses a phased array of laser emitters spread over about a kilometre, firing in step as one huge mirror. With 100 GW pushing a 4-metre sail, the craft would reach about a fifth of the speed of light within minutes (published estimates range from a few to about ten), by which time it is millions of kilometres away, several times farther than the Moon, and the beam has spread too wide to help.

A wider array keeps the beam tight for longer; a lighter craft accelerates faster. Every gram matters: the probe itself, a “StarChip”, would be a wafer-scale computer with camera, sensors and a laser transmitter, all in about a gram.

The math
Dspot ≈ 2.44 λL / Darray,  F = (2Phit/c) (1 − β)/(1 + β),  mγ³ dv/dt = F

Integrated step by step: λ = 1.06 µm, a 4 m sail, and the share of power hitting the sail falling as (Dsail/Dspot)² once the spot outgrows it. The (1 − β)/(1 + β) factor is the relativistic Doppler effect: at high speed, reflected photons are redshifted and deliver less push. Real beams are fuzzier than this sharp cut-off, and atmospheric turbulence must be corrected.

03The long cruise

Twenty years to another star

Alpha Centauri is 4.37 light-years away. Voyager 1, our fastest probe leaving the solar system, would take about 77,000 years to cover that. At a fifth of the speed of light a sail would do it in about 22 years, and a picture of the planet Proxima b (Φ 023) would take another 4.2 years to reach us.

The cruise is perilous. At 60,000 km/s, a speck of interstellar dust a hundredth of a millimetre across strikes with the energy of a rifle bullet. The probes would fly edge-on to present the smallest target, and many would be launched, since each costs little once the laser is built.

Phoning home is just as hard: a gram-scale probe must aim a laser signal of a watt or so across four light-years at a telescope on Earth. Starshot was announced in 2016 by Yuri Milner and Stephen Hawking as a research programme, and its studies continue; the laser array alone would be among the largest engineering projects ever attempted.

Observations

Sailing so far

1610 · 1924

The idea

Kepler, watching comet tails stream away from the Sun, imagined ships with sails for the heavenly breeze. In the 1920s Friedrich Zander and Konstantin Tsiolkovsky proposed sails pushed by sunlight.

2010

IKAROS

The Japanese space agency’s 20-metre sail, thinner than a human hair, was the first spacecraft propelled by sunlight, on its way to Venus.

2019

LightSail 2

A crowd-funded CubeSat that raised its own orbit using only sunlight on a 32 m² sail, for over three years.

2016–

Breakthrough Starshot

A research programme exploring gram-scale laser-driven probes to Alpha Centauri. Laboratory work on ultrathin, highly reflective sail materials and beam stability continues.
