zero-point energy

FRINGE SCIENCE · energy from the vacuum — the claim that the restless energy of empty space can be tapped as a power source

Quantum theory says nothing can ever be perfectly still. Even in empty space at absolute zero, every field keeps a minimum jitter called zero-point energy. That much is mainstream physics, and the vacuum’s effects have been measured again and again: it pushes metal plates together, shifts the energy levels of atoms, and can even be shaken into producing real light. Advocates such as the physicist Harold Puthoff argue that a resource this real, and this large, will one day be engineered. Here you can feel the vacuum push, shake light out of nothing, and see a device that claims to draw current from it.

01Casimir, 1948 · Lamoreaux, 1997

Empty space pushes

Pressure from the vacuum—
Compared with air pressure—
Measured precision—

In 1948 the Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir predicted something astonishing. Put two uncharged metal plates very close together in a vacuum. Between them, only zero-point waves that fit the gap can exist; outside, every wavelength is allowed. The extra waves outside push the plates together.

No charge, no gravity, no air: the force comes from the energy of empty space itself.

The case forSteve Lamoreaux measured the Casimir force in 1997 to within about 5% of the prediction, and later experiments reached 1%. At a gap of ten nanometres it equals about one atmosphere of pressure, strong enough that engineers must design micro-machines to stop their parts sticking. In 2009 Federico Capasso’s group at Harvard showed in Nature that with the right materials the force can even be made repulsive. Advocates argue that a force we can measure, reverse and engineer is a force we can learn to harvest.
The case againstThe Casimir force behaves like a spring: closing the plates releases energy and separating them costs the same, so no cycle has been shown to come out ahead.
Try thisClose the gap from a micrometre down to ten nanometres and watch the pressure climb sixteen-fold for every halving of the gap.
The math
P = π² ħ c / (240 d4)

Casimir’s formula for ideal metal plates; at d = 10 nm it gives about 1.3 × 105 Pa. Real metals give somewhat less at the smallest gaps.

Shaking light out of nothing

In the experiment—

Theory predicted in 1970 that a mirror moving fast enough through the vacuum would turn virtual zero-point photons into real ones, light created from empty space. It seemed impossible to test: no real mirror can be shaken anywhere near the speed of light.

Christopher Wilson, Per Delsing and colleagues at Chalmers University in Sweden found a way. They built an “electrical mirror” from a superconducting circuit whose effective position could be changed billions of times a second.

The case forReported in Nature in 2011, the circuit emitted microwave photons in correlated pairs, exactly as predicted for light pulled out of the vacuum. Energy from the drive was turned into real radiation through the zero-point field. Advocates see this as the first demonstration that vacuum fluctuations can be converted into usable energy with the right moving boundary.
The case againstThe energy of the photons comes from the work done driving the mirror, so the vacuum acts as a medium, not a source of free power.
Try thisSpeed up the mirror and watch photon pairs appear; the rate grows with the square of the speed.
The math
photon production rate ∝ (v / c)²

For a mirror oscillating at frequency Ω with peak speed v, pairs are created at frequencies adding up to Ω. The slider shows peak speeds up to a few percent of the speed of light, the regime of the Chalmers circuit.

03Boulder, 2021

A diode inside a Casimir cavity

Vacuum modes suppressed—
Control devices—

If the vacuum pushes harder on one side of something than the other, perhaps electrons can be pushed too. Garret Moddel, an electrical engineering professor at the University of Colorado Boulder, put that idea to the test.

His group built tiny metal-insulator-metal diodes and placed a thin optical cavity, a Casimir cavity, on one side, so that some zero-point fluctuations were suppressed there but not on the other side.

The case forIn 2021 the team reported in the journal Symmetry that devices with Casimir cavities produced a small, steady electric current with no applied power, while otherwise identical devices without cavities did not. The current depended on the cavity in the way an imbalance of vacuum fluctuations would predict. If it holds up, it is the first device to draw continuous electrical power from the structure of the vacuum.
The case againstThe currents are tiny, the results have not been independently replicated, and thermal or contact effects that mimic a vacuum source are hard to rule out.
Try thisMake the cavity thinner and watch the current rise. Switch to the control device: no cavity, no current.
The math
current ∝ fraction of vacuum modes suppressed on one side

The curve is schematic: thinner cavities suppress more long-wavelength modes, so the imbalance and the reported current grow. Values are shown in arbitrary units, not the published figures.

Observations

Milestones

1947

The Lamb shift

Willis Lamb measured a tiny shift in hydrogen’s energy levels caused by vacuum fluctuations, earning a Nobel Prize.

1948

Casimir’s prediction

Working at Philips, Hendrik Casimir calculated that the vacuum pushes neutral plates together.

1994

Inertia from the vacuum

Bernard Haisch, Alfonso Rueda and Harold Puthoff proposed in Physical Review A that inertia itself arises from the zero-point field.

2011

Light from nothing

The dynamical Casimir effect was observed at Chalmers University, creating real photons from the vacuum.
