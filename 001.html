eigenfunction

GERMAN · eigen — one’s own, characteristic

Act on almost anything and it changes shape. A rare few only grow, shrink or flip. Those survivors are the native alphabet of physics: the notes of a string, the patterns of a drum, the energy levels of an atom.

Take the flat plane and transform it: stretch it, shear it, flip it. Draw any arrow and it comes out pointing somewhere new.

Almost any arrow. Along a few special lines, an arrow comes out exactly on the line it started on, only longer, shorter or reversed. These are the eigenvectors. The factor each one is scaled by is its eigenvalue, written λ.

The eigenvectors are the transformation’s skeleton. Know them and their λ, and you know everything the transformation does: every other arrow is just a blend of them.

Try thisDrag inside the field to aim the probe. Hunt down the lines where it stops turning. Then try Rotate, and see why a pure rotation has none.
The math
Av = λv

A matrix A acting on an eigenvector v gives back the same vector times a number λ. To find the λ values, solve det(A − λI) = 0. For a 2×2 matrix that is a quadratic. Two real roots give two eigen-lines (Stretch, Flip). A repeated root can leave one (Shear). Complex roots mean every real arrow turns (Rotate).

A function is an arrow with infinitely many components, one for every point along it. Operations on functions are called operators, and they can have eigenvectors too. We call those eigenfunctions.

The operator that rules a guitar string is curvature: how sharply the string bends at each point. Curvature is what pulls the string back toward straight.

Feed it one of these smooth standing waves and the curvature (dotted) comes back as the same wave, upside-down and scaled. So each standing wave just swings up and down with its shape intact, its nodes frozen in place. That is an eigenfunction in motion.

The arrows make the link to Bay 01 literal. Sample the string at a handful of points and it becomes a list of numbers: an ordinary vector, one pink arrow per component. The curvature operator becomes a matrix that compares each point with its two neighbours. For a standing wave, the violet arrows it returns are the pink ones flipped and scaled by one number, λ. Add more sample points and the vector turns into the function.

Now pluck it. A pluck is a mixture of many standing waves, each swinging at its own rate, so the shape smears and travels. The bars underneath show the recipe. Those extra ingredients are the overtones you hear in every real note.

Try thisWatch the Shape-kept meter on n = 1 to 5, then drag the string anywhere to pluck it and watch the meter fall. Slide the sample points from 6 to 40 and watch λ from the vector close in on −(nπ)².
The math
d²fdx² = λf,  f(0) = f(L) = 0

The pinned ends allow only fn(x) = sin(nπx/L), with λn = −(nπ/L)². The wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c²∂²u/∂x² then makes mode n oscillate at n times the fundamental frequency. Any pluck is a Fourier sum Σ bnfn, and each term evolves on its own.

(Av)i = (vi−1 − 2vi + vi+1) / h²,  λn = −(4/h²) sin²(nπh/2)

With N sample points spaced h = L/(N + 1), curvature is an N × N matrix. The sampled standing waves are its exact eigenvectors, and its eigenvalues approach −(nπ/L)² as N grows. The readout computes λ = (Av · v) / (v · v) from the arrows themselves, here with L = 1.

Give the string a second dimension and you have a drumhead. Its eigenfunctions are labelled by two whole numbers, (m, n). The pink lines are nodes: they never move while the skin around them heaves.

The pitches go up as √(m² + n²), which is not a neat musical ladder. That is why a drum thuds where a string sings.

The arrows sample the surface on a grid, turning it into one long vector with a component at every grid point. The violet arrows are the 2D curvature operator ∇² applied to that vector, divided by the size of λ so the two can be compared. For a single mode each violet arrow is its pink partner turned upside down: the same vector times a negative number. Superpose two modes and the pairs no longer match.

Now switch to Quantum box. Replace the drumhead with an electron trapped in a square box and the very same shapes reappear. Schrödinger’s equation, Ĥψ = Eψ, is an eigenvalue problem. Its eigenfunctions are the states of definite energy, and its eigenvalues are the energies themselves.

In a single eigenstate the probability cloud does not move at all, which is why physicists call these states stationary. Superpose two and the cloud starts to slosh. Because only whole-number (m, n) fit in the box, the energy comes in steps. That is the “quantum” in quantum mechanics.

Try thisPick a mode and check that every violet arrow mirrors its pink partner. Press Superpose and watch them disagree. Then switch to Quantum box and watch nothing move.
The math
∇²φ = −k²φ,  φmn = sin(mπx/L) sin(nπy/L)

Drum: ωmn = (cπ/L)√(m² + n²).

Ĥψ = Eψ,  Ĥ = −ħ²2M∇²

Box: Emn = (ħ²π²/2ML²)(m² + n²). Each eigenstate carries a phase e−iEt/ħ, which cancels in |ψ|², so the density is constant. Two states with different E interfere, and the density oscillates at (E₁ − E₂)/ħ.

(∇²v)ij = (vi±1,j + vi,j±1 − 4vij) / h²

The five-point Laplacian on a G × G grid with L = 1. Sampled modes are its exact eigenvectors, with λmn = −(4/h²)[sin²(mπh/2) + sin²(nπh/2)] → −π²(m² + n²). In Quantum box the arrows show the real part of ψ, which swings in time while |ψ|² stays put.

Observations

Where you will meet them next

Atomic physics

The shape of an atom

The s, p, d and f orbitals in a chemistry textbook are eigenfunctions of hydrogen’s energy operator. Chemistry’s periodic table is built on their eigenvalues.

Gravitational waves

A black hole’s ringdown

After two black holes merge, the new one rings like a struck bell in a set of damped modes, close cousins of eigenmodes. LIGO reads the final mass and spin from their frequencies.

The web

Google’s first ranking

PageRank scored every page using the leading eigenvector of the web’s link matrix: the one arrow the endless shuffle of clicks leaves pointing the same way.

Machine learning

The directions data cares about

Principal component analysis finds the eigenvectors of a dataset’s covariance matrix. The largest eigenvalues mark the few directions that hold most of the variation.

Note to self

An eigenfunction is a shape an operation can only rescale. Find them, and one tangled problem splits into many simple ones that each run on their own.
