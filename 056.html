morphic resonance

FRINGE SCIENCE · nature’s memory — the proposal that forms and habits are shaped by fields carrying a collective memory of everything similar that came before

In 1981 the biologist Rupert Sheldrake, a former research fellow of Clare College, Cambridge, and of the Royal Society, proposed that nature has habits. Once rats somewhere learn a trick, rats everywhere should learn it a little faster; once a new chemical crystallises, it should crystallise more easily around the world. The idea explains a cluster of puzzles that standard biology leaves open, and unlike many fringe claims it makes predictions anyone can test. Here you can watch rats improve across fifty generations, follow a new crystal form around the world, and test whether old rhymes are easier to learn than new ones.

011920–1954

Rats that learned across generations

Trained line, first → last generation—
Control line, first → last generation—
What inheritance alone predicts for controls—

From 1920 the Harvard psychologist William McDougall trained rats to escape a water tank by the darker of two exits, then bred from them. Over 32 generations later rats learned faster. W. E. Agar in Melbourne repeated the experiment for 50 generations, adding a crucial control: a line bred only from untrained rats.

The control rats got faster too. Their parents had never seen the tank, so ordinary inheritance cannot explain it. Sheldrake saw exactly the signature of morphic resonance: every rat, related or not, drawing on the growing memory of all the rats that had learned before.

The case forThe improvement appeared in both lines and lasted decades, on two continents. In a third replication in Edinburgh, F. A. E. Crew found that his rats made few errors from the very first generation, as if they had inherited the habit from rats far away. Sheldrake points out that the pattern was found by researchers who were testing a different idea entirely, so it can’t have been produced by expectation of morphic resonance.
The case againstAgar’s rats also varied with the health of the colony, and lab handling changed over decades; without simultaneous isolated colonies, these effects are hard to separate.
Try thisLook at the control line with the morphic effect on, then set it to zero: with inheritance alone, the controls should stay flat. Agar’s controls did not.
The math
errors(g) = E0 × (1 − m × (1 − e−g/20)) + noise

Here m is the morphic effect, shared by both lines because it depends on how many rats have learned, not on ancestry. The trained line also gets a small extra improvement from selection. The curves are illustrative, not Agar’s data.

02Disappearing crystals

A new form goes global

Labs now making the new form—
Reached by contact—
Isolated lab—

Chemists know a strange pattern well: newly made compounds are often hard to crystallise at first, then become easier everywhere over the following years. Sometimes a new crystal form appears and the old one can no longer be made anywhere, a phenomenon called a disappearing polymorph.

The most famous case is ritonavir, an HIV drug. In 1998, two years after launch, a new, less soluble crystal form appeared, and factories could no longer produce the original. The drug had to be withdrawn and reformulated.

The case forSheldrake predicted this pattern from morphic resonance in 1981, before the ritonavir case. Crystallographers Jack Dunitz and Joel Bernstein reviewed many disappearing polymorphs in 1995 and noted that new forms can appear in labs separated by long distances within short periods, sometimes with no known contact. A field-like memory explains simultaneous appearance; contamination needs a physical carrier for every case.
The case againstMicroscopic seed crystals spread easily on clothes, equipment and in the air, and chemists usually blame seeding; a strictly isolated laboratory has rarely been tried.
Try thisWatch the new form spread, and keep an eye on the isolated lab at the bottom. Set the morphic effect to zero: now it can only spread by contact, and the isolated lab never converts.
The math
P(convert per step) = contact × seeded neighbours + m × (share of labs already converted)

Each step, a lab can be seeded by a connected lab that already makes the new form, or converted by the morphic term, which grows with how much of the new form exists worldwide. The isolated lab has no contacts.

03Learning tests

Old rhymes or new ones?

Traditional rhyme: lines recalled—
New rhyme: lines recalled—
Odds of the difference by luck—

Sheldrake proposed a simple test: a rhyme recited by millions of people for generations should carry a strong morphic field, and should be easier to learn than a new one, even for people who don’t speak the language. Volunteers learn both, without knowing which is which.

The new rhyme is composed to match the old one in length, rhythm and sound, so only its history differs.

The case forSheldrake reported that English speakers learned a traditional Japanese nursery rhyme more easily than newly composed ones. At Yale, the psychologist Gary Schwartz found that people who knew no Hebrew rated common Hebrew words as feeling more familiar than rare ones, though both looked equally foreign to them. Proponents note that these effects appeared in independent labs using blinded designs.
The case againstResults have been mixed, many studies were small, and the advantage depended on how well the control material was matched; no large pre-registered test has found a clear effect.
Try thisRun a few groups of volunteers with the effect at 12%, then set it to zero. Notice how much a small advantage needs a decent sample to stand out.
The math
z = (meanold − meannew) / √(2σ² / n)

Forty simulated volunteers each learn eight lines of both rhymes; each line is recalled with probability 0.45, raised by the morphic effect for the traditional rhyme.
