uncertainty

QUANTUM · uncertainty principle — the sharper a particle’s position, the blurrier its motion, and the reverse

This isn’t about clumsy instruments. A particle is a wave, and a wave cannot have a sharp position and a sharp wavelength at the same time. That one fact sets the size of every atom in your body.

01The trade-off

Build a particle out of waves

Position spread Δx—
Momentum spread Δp—
Δx × Δp—

A pure wave with one exact wavelength runs on forever. It has a perfectly sharp momentum, because in quantum mechanics wavelength is momentum. But it is everywhere at once, with no position at all.

To make something localised, a lump you could call a particle, you have to add many waves with slightly different wavelengths. They reinforce in one region and cancel everywhere else. The more wavelengths you mix in, the tighter the lump.

That is the whole uncertainty principle. A tight position needs a wide mix of wavelengths, which means a blurry momentum. A sharp momentum needs a long, spread-out wave. You can trade one for the other, but you can never shrink both.

Werner Heisenberg stated the principle in 1927. The product of the two spreads can never fall below a fixed amount, set by Planck’s constant.

Try thisDrag the mix from narrow to wide and watch the packet shrink while the momentum bars spread. The product readout stays pinned at its minimum, ½ in these units.
The math
Δx · Δp ≥ ħ2,  p = ħk = hλ

The packet is ψ(x) = Σ A(k) eikx over about 120 waves, with Gaussian weights A(k) of width Δk. A Gaussian is the shape that reaches the limit exactly: Δx = 1/(2Δk). Mathematically, position and momentum are Fourier transforms of each other. The Kennard inequality (1927) is a theorem about Fourier transforms.

02Pin it down

Squeeze a particle and it flies apart

Located to within—
Minimum speed blur—
Blur after 1 second—

Suppose you measure a particle’s position precisely. You have forced its wave into a narrow lump, and by Bay 01 that lump is made of a wide mix of wavelengths, meaning a wide range of possible speeds.

So the lump immediately starts to spread. The faster parts run ahead and the slower ones lag behind. The more tightly you pinned it, the faster it flies apart. Compare the tightly located particle with the loosely located one.

For an electron this is dramatic. Pin one down to the width of an atom and its speed becomes uncertain by hundreds of kilometres per second. For a grain of sand the effect still exists, but it is far too small ever to notice. That is why the everyday world looks sharp.

Try thisSqueeze the electron down and read the speed blur. Then switch to a grain of sand at the same precision and compare the two numbers.
The math
σ(t) = σ₀ √1 + (ħt / 2mσ₀²)²

A free Gaussian packet’s width grows like this. The narrower the start σ₀, the faster the growth. The readouts use Δv = ħ/(2mΔx), the smallest possible speed spread. Masses used: electron 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, proton 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, a virus about 10⁻¹⁸ kg, a grain of sand about 10⁻⁶ kg.

03Why atoms have a size

The squeeze that holds every atom open

Squeeze energy—
Electric pull—
Total energy—

A hydrogen atom is a proton and an electron that attract each other. Classically the electron should spiral into the proton, radiating light all the way in, in about 16 trillionths of a second. Every atom in the universe would collapse.

The uncertainty principle forbids it. Squeeze the electron’s cloud smaller and you blur its momentum, giving it more kinetic energy. Close in, that squeeze energy grows faster than the electric pull can pay for it.

The atom settles where the two balance. The calculation gives a size of 0.053 nanometres and an energy of −13.6 electronvolts, the measured values for hydrogen. A rough argument from uncertainty lands on the exact answer.

So the size of atoms, and everything built from them, comes from this trade-off. You are mostly empty space held open by the uncertainty principle.

Try thisSqueeze the cloud small and watch the squeeze energy shoot up. Expand it and the atom loses its grip. Then press Let it settle and see where it comes to rest.
The math
E(r) ≈ ħ²2mer² − e²4πε₀r

Confining the electron to size r gives momentum p ~ ħ/r, so kinetic energy ~ ħ²/2mr². Minimising E gives r = 4πε₀ħ²/mee² = 0.0529 nm, the Bohr radius, and E = −13.6 eV. In these units: E = 0.0381/r² − 1.440/r eV, with r in nm.

Observations

The trade-off at work

Music

A short note has no exact pitch

The same mathematics governs sound. A sharp click contains every frequency, while a pure tone must ring for a while. Sound engineers know this as the Gabor limit.

Gravitational waves

Squeezed light in LIGO

You can’t beat the limit, but you can choose where the uncertainty goes. LIGO injects “squeezed” light with less noise in the quantity it measures and more in the one it ignores, extending its reach into space.

Cold matter

Helium that never freezes

At normal pressure, helium stays liquid down to absolute zero. Its atoms are so light that the jiggling forced by the uncertainty principle, called zero-point motion, is enough to stop them locking into a solid.

A common myth

Not about clumsy measurement

Heisenberg first explained it as a measurement disturbing the particle. The modern view is deeper: the particle doesn’t have both sharp values to disturb in the first place.

Note to self

A wave can’t be in one place and have one wavelength at once. Everything is made of waves, so nothing can be perfectly still or perfectly located.
