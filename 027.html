fusion power

BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · energy — bottling a small star, and why it has taken 70 years

Fuse two forms of hydrogen and you release about four times more energy per kilogram than splitting uranium, using deuterium from seawater and tritium bred from lithium, with no carbon and no long-lived waste. The catch: the fuel must be hotter than the Sun’s core and held together long enough to burn, inside a cage made of nothing but magnetic fields.

01Hotter than the Sun

Why it takes 150 million degrees

Temperature—
Fusion happens at about—
Fusion rate vs 150 million °C—

The easiest fusion reaction joins deuterium and tritium, two heavy forms of hydrogen, into helium and a fast neutron, releasing 17.6 MeV. Both nuclei are positively charged, so they repel. Pushing them close enough to touch would take energies of hundreds of keV.

Two things help. In a hot gas a few nuclei are moving much faster than average (pink). And nuclei don’t have to climb all the way over the barrier: they can tunnel through it (Φ 002), with a chance that rises steeply with energy (ice). Multiply the two and you get a narrow window, the Gamow peak, where almost all fusion happens.

The Sun’s core, at 15 million °C, gets away with being cooler because it is enormous and patient. On Earth we need fusion fast, which means around 150 million °C, ten times hotter than the Sun’s core.

02The magnetic bottle

A cage made of field lines

Particles still confined—
How they escape—

No material wall survives 150 million degrees, so the plasma must not touch one. Charged particles in a magnetic field spiral tightly around the field lines and can’t easily cross them. That gives you a bottle, but a leaky one.

In a straight tube the particles simply run out of the ends. Bend the tube into a ring and there are no ends, but the field is stronger on the inside of the bend, which makes ions drift one way and electrons the other. The charges separate, an electric field builds up, and the whole plasma is pushed outward into the wall.

The fix, worked out by Soviet physicists in the 1950s, is to drive a current through the plasma itself so the field lines twist around the ring like stripes on a candy cane. Each particle then spends as much time above the centre as below it, and the drifts cancel. This is the tokamak. Its cousin, the stellarator, gets the twist from intricately shaped coils instead.

Try thisTry each field shape in turn. Watch the cross-section on the right: in the plain ring the particles march off up and down; in the tokamak they circle on closed loops.
The math
m dv/dt = q v × B,  vdrift ≈ (v∥² + ½v⊥²) / (ΩR)

Each particle is pushed with the Boris method through a ring-shaped field falling off as 1/R, plus, for the tokamak, a twisting field from the plasma current (safety factor about 2). Particles are drawn hundreds of times larger than real, to make the spirals and drifts visible; real ions gyrate in millimetres inside a ring several metres across. The ring view shows only the vertical drift; the electric field that then flings the plasma outward is described, not simulated.

03Getting more out than in

The Lawson criterion

Triple product—
Gain Q—
Verdict—

A plasma constantly leaks heat. To keep it hot you either keep heating it or let the fusion heat it. In D–T fusion a fifth of the energy comes out as helium nuclei that stay trapped and heat the plasma; the rest leaves as neutrons, which a power plant would catch to boil water.

In 1955 John Lawson worked out what it takes: the plasma must be hot, dense and well insulated (a long energy confinement time) all at once. Their product must pass about 3 × 10²¹ keV·s/m³. The gain Q compares fusion power with the heating power you supply. Q = 1 is break-even; at ignition the plasma keeps itself hot.

In 2021 Britain’s JET tokamak produced 59 megajoules in five seconds, and in its final 2023 run, 69. In December 2022 the US National Ignition Facility, which crushes tiny fuel pellets with lasers instead of using magnets, got more fusion energy out of a pellet (3.15 MJ) than the laser light put in (2.05 MJ), the first time ever. ITER in France aims for Q = 10.

The math
Pfus = ¼ n² ⟨σv⟩ Efus,  Ploss = 3nT/τE,  Q = Pfus / (Ploss − Pα)

A 50/50 D–T plasma with ion and electron temperatures equal, losing heat only through the confinement time; radiation and impurities are ignored, which makes this optimistic. Pα = Pfus/5. NIF’s gain compares fusion energy with laser light on target; the lasers themselves drew far more from the grid. Engineering gain for a whole power plant is a much tougher target.

Observations

Machines to know

Culham, UK · 1983–2023

JET

The Joint European Torus, for decades the world’s most powerful tokamak. Its final deuterium–tritium run set the record for energy from a fusion experiment: 69 MJ.

Cadarache, France

ITER

A 35-nation tokamak, the largest ever built, designed to produce 500 MW from 50 MW of heating. Delays have pushed full deuterium–tritium operation into the late 2030s.

Massachusetts, USA

SPARC

A compact tokamak from Commonwealth Fusion Systems using high-temperature superconducting magnets (Φ 028) to get much stronger fields in a much smaller machine. It aims to show Q > 1.

Greifswald, Germany

Wendelstein 7-X

The most advanced stellarator, whose twisted coils were designed by supercomputer. It holds long pulses without needing a current driven through the plasma.

Note to self

Fusion needs plasma that is hot, dense and well insulated all at once; a twisted magnetic bottle can hold it, and the race now is to get much more energy out than goes in.
