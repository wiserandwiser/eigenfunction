gravitational waves

ASTROPHYSICS · general relativity in motion — when heavy things whirl around each other, space itself ripples, and we can now feel it

Einstein’s gravity says mass curves spacetime (Φ 003). Shake that mass and the curvature can’t update everywhere at once: the change travels outward at the speed of light, as a wave that stretches and squeezes space itself. In 2015, a pair of detectors in Louisiana and Washington felt one for the first time.

01The chirp

Two black holes spiral in and merge

Wave frequency—
Time to merger—
Separation—

Two orbiting black holes churn spacetime and send out gravitational waves. The waves carry away orbital energy, so the pair sinks closer, which makes them orbit faster, which makes the waves stronger. The process runs away.

The wave’s frequency is twice the orbital frequency, and it sweeps upward faster and faster: a chirp. In the last fifth of a second, black holes each about 30 times the Sun’s mass were whirling around each other at about half the speed of light. Then they merged into one black hole that “rang” like a struck bell and fell silent.

That merger, detected on 14 September 2015, released about three Suns’ worth of mass as pure gravitational-wave energy in a fraction of a second, more power than all the stars in the observable universe put together. Neutron stars, being lighter, chirp for much longer and to a higher pitch.

τ is the time left before merger and ℳ the “chirp mass”, which detectors measure directly from how fast the pitch rises. The readouts use this leading-order formula from 30 Hz upward and stop it at the merger; the ringdown is a decaying tone at the final black hole’s natural frequency. The neutron-star signal has thousands of cycles in band; it is drawn with far fewer. Hear it plays the black-hole chirp about four times slower and two octaves higher so small speakers can carry it, and squeezes the neutron-star chirp into 3 seconds.

02Feeling it

Space stretches, and a laser ruler notices

Arm stretch drawn—
Real stretch, 4 km arm—
Compared with a proton—

A gravitational wave doesn’t push things. It changes the distances between them. As it passes, space stretches along one direction while squeezing along the other, then swaps, over and over. A ring of floating particles wobbles into an oval and back.

The effect is absurdly small. The 2015 wave stretched the Earth by about the width of an atom’s nucleus, and briefly. To feel it, the LIGO detectors use two 4-km arms at right angles. A laser is split down both arms, bounced off mirrors hanging on pendulums, and recombined. Normally the two beams cancel and the output is dark. When a wave lengthens one arm and shortens the other, the beams fall out of step and light leaks out.

The change in arm length was about a few thousandths of a proton’s width. Measuring it took decades of work isolating the mirrors from earthquakes, traffic, ocean waves and the jiggle of their own atoms.

Try thisTurn the exaggeration down and watch the oval, the arms and the output light change together. Switch to Cross: the same wave rotated by 45°, which this detector barely notices.
The math
ΔL/L = h ,  h ~ 10−21,  Pout ∝ sin²(2π ΔL / λ)

h is the strain, the fractional change in length. The readouts take the peak strain of the 2015 event, about 1.0 × 10⁻²¹, on a 4-km arm. The real detectors fold the light back and forth about 300 times and add power recycling to make the tiny phase shift measurable. An L-shaped detector responds most to the plus pattern lined up with its arms.

03A new sky

Bigger pairs, lower notes

Merger note—
Heard by—
Example—

The heavier the pair, the larger its final orbit and the lower its note. Merging stellar black holes and neutron stars sing at tens to thousands of hertz, which ground detectors (LIGO, Virgo, KAGRA) can hear. By 2025 they had logged around 300 candidate mergers.

Black holes millions of times the Sun’s mass, at the centres of colliding galaxies, hum at millihertz: one cycle every few minutes to hours. Ground detectors can’t hear that over the Earth’s own rumbling, so LISA, three spacecraft millions of kilometres apart, is planned for launch in the 2030s.

The biggest pairs, billions of Suns, take years per cycle. No laser can be that long. Instead, astronomers time dozens of pulsars across the galaxy as a clock network, and in 2023 several teams reported evidence of this slow background hum (Φ 020).

Observations

Landmark signals

1974

The Hulse–Taylor pulsar

A pulsar orbiting a neutron star, whose orbit shrinks by about 3.5 metres a year, exactly as gravitational-wave losses predict. The first indirect proof that the waves exist, and a 1993 Nobel Prize.

2015

GW150914

The first direct detection: two black holes of about 36 and 29 Suns, 1.3 billion light-years away. The signal hit Livingston 7 milliseconds before Hanford. Nobel Prize, 2017.

2017

GW170817

Two neutron stars, heard for about 100 seconds and then seen as a kilonova and a gamma-ray burst arriving 1.7 s later, proving gravity travels at the speed of light (Φ 018).

2023

The nanohertz hum

Pulsar timing arrays in North America, Europe, Australia and China reported evidence of a background of very slow waves, most likely from supermassive black-hole pairs across the universe.
