noether’s symmetry

PARTICLES & FORCES · symmetry and conservation — every symmetry of the laws of nature comes with something that can never be created or destroyed

In 1918 Emmy Noether proved what may be the most important theorem in physics. If the laws are the same everywhere, momentum is conserved. If they are the same in every direction, angular momentum is conserved. If they are the same at all times, energy is conserved. A century of particle physics has been a hunt for symmetries, and for the places where they break.

01Noether’s theorem

Change the world, change what’s conserved

Momentum—
Angular momentum—
Energy—

A symmetry is something you can do to a situation that leaves its laws unchanged. Slide the whole experiment a metre to the left, turn it to face north, run it tomorrow instead of today: if nothing about the physics changes, that is a symmetry.

Noether showed that each continuous symmetry guarantees a conserved quantity. Empty space looks the same everywhere, so momentum is conserved. A round bowl looks the same from every angle around its centre, so angular momentum is conserved, but momentum is not: the bowl pushes things around. A tilted floor is the same if you slide sideways, so only sideways momentum survives.

Energy is the conserved quantity that comes from time. Make the bowl pulse, so the laws change from moment to moment, and energy is no longer conserved. On the largest scales the expanding universe is not the same at all times, which is why light loses energy as it stretches (Φ 026).

If the Lagrangian L (kinetic minus potential energy) does not change when a coordinate q shifts, the matching momentum is conserved. Position gives momentum, angle gives angular momentum, time gives energy. 02Broken symmetry

Nature fails the mirror test

Real world—
Mirror world—
Verdict—

Physicists long assumed nature can’t tell left from right: the mirror image of any experiment should be an experiment that could really happen. In 1956 Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang pointed out that nobody had checked this for the weak force.

Chien-Shiung Wu checked within months. She chilled cobalt-60 to a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero and lined up its nuclear spins with a magnetic coil. The electrons from its radioactive decay came out mostly opposite to the spin.

Now look in a mirror. The coil’s current runs the other way round, so the spin points the other way, but the electrons still head the same way. In the mirror world, electrons go along the spin. That world would contradict what Wu saw: the weak force is left-handed, and the mirror is not a symmetry of nature.

Try thisWatch the counts build up on both sides, then reverse the coil. Switch on the mirror-fair law to see what physicists had expected.
The math
W(θ) ∝ 1 + A cos θ,  A ≈ −0.4 here

θ is the angle between the electron and the nuclear spin. Spin behaves like rotation, so a mirror reverses it while leaving the electron’s direction alone, turning A into −A. Wu measured an asymmetry of this size, smaller than the full theoretical value because her nuclei were only partly aligned. Mirror symmetry is a flip, not a smooth change, so it has no Noether quantity, but its breaking is just as telling.

03Prediction

A particle found by symmetry

Step between rows—
Symmetry predicts—
Measured—

By 1960 accelerators had found dozens of new particles, a confusing “zoo”. Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne’eman noticed that, sorted by charge and strangeness, they fell into neat geometric patterns, the Eightfold Way. The pattern came from a symmetry among three kinds of quark, though quarks themselves were not proposed until 1964.

One pattern, a triangle of ten, had nine known members and an empty corner. Each row down was heavier by almost the same step, about 150 MeV, the extra mass of one more strange quark. In 1962 Gell-Mann predicted the missing particle: charge −1, strangeness −3, mass about 1685 MeV.

In 1964 a bubble-chamber photograph at Brookhaven showed it: the omega-minus, at 1672 MeV. Symmetry had found a particle before anyone saw it, just as it would later point to the W, Z and Higgs.

Observations

Milestones

1918

Noether’s theorem

Emmy Noether, working in Göttingen without pay because she was a woman, proved the link between symmetry and conservation.

1956–57

The mirror breaks

Chien-Shiung Wu’s cobalt-60 experiment showed the weak force tells left from right. Lee and Yang won the 1957 Nobel Prize; Wu did not.

1964

The omega-minus

Brookhaven’s bubble chamber caught the particle the Eightfold Way had predicted, convincing physicists that symmetry was the key to the particle zoo.

1964

CP violation

James Cronin and Val Fitch found that even mirror-plus-antimatter symmetry fails slightly, one ingredient for the survival of matter (Φ 038).

Note to self

Every symmetry of the laws gives something conserved: space gives momentum, direction gives angular momentum, time gives energy, and broken symmetries tell us just as much.
