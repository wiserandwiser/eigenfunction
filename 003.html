spacetime

GEOMETRY · spacetime — space and time as one fabric, which mass can bend

Newton said gravity is a force that pulls. Einstein said nothing pulls at all. Mass bends space and time, and a falling object is simply moving as straight as it can through that bent geometry. Surprisingly, most of the work is done by bending time.

Two travellers stand on the equator a short way apart. Both walk due north. Neither ever turns. Yet they drift closer together, and they meet at the pole.

Nothing pulls them together. The surface is curved, and on a curved surface, straight lines that start out parallel do not stay parallel.

The straightest possible path across a surface is called a geodesic. On a flat surface, geodesics that start parallel stay parallel forever. Set the curvature to zero and see.

Einstein’s idea was that mass curves spacetime, and anything in free fall, like a dropped apple, an orbiting moon or the Earth itself, is simply following a geodesic. What we feel as the pull of gravity is curvature, the same thing that brought the travellers together.

Try thisChoose Flat, then slowly raise the curvature. Watch the gap start to close. A sharper curve makes them meet sooner, however far apart they start.
The math
d²ξds² = −K ξ  ⇒  ξ(s) = ξ₀ cos(√K s)

The geodesic deviation (Jacobi) equation. On a surface with curvature K = 1/R², the gap ξ between neighbouring geodesics shrinks like a cosine and closes after a distance πR/2. In general relativity the same idea uses the Riemann curvature tensor:

D²ξμdτ² = −Rμνρσ uνξρuσ

That relative squeeze between free-falling neighbours is the tidal force.

Mass does not only curve space. It slows time. A clock closer to a mass ticks slower than one higher up.

This has been measured, not just predicted. Optical atomic clocks raised by only 33 cm have been caught running faster than their twins below (NIST, 2010).

This warping of time is most of what gravity is. For anything moving slowly, like a dropped apple or a thrown ball, the bending of time does nearly all the work. The straightest path through spacetime curves toward where clocks run slower, and we call that falling.

Your phone depends on it. GPS satellites orbit 20,200 km up. Their clocks gain about 45 microseconds a day from the weaker gravity and lose about 7 from their speed. Left uncorrected, the net 38 microseconds would throw your position off by roughly 10 km every day.

Try thisSet the high clock to GPS height and read the gain. Then pick Neutron star and watch the ground clock visibly fall behind. Finally, hover beside a black hole.
The math
dτdt = √1 − rs/r,  rs = 2GMc²

A clock hovering at radius r ticks at this rate compared with a clock far away. rs is the Schwarzschild radius: 8.9 mm for the Earth, 3 km for the Sun. Near the ground this reduces to Δτ/τ ≈ gh/c², about one part in 10¹⁶ per metre of height.

The readouts show only this gravitational effect for clocks held still. Real GPS satellites also move fast, which special relativity says slows them by about 7 µs a day.

Curve space and time around one compact mass and you get the Schwarzschild geometry. Karl Schwarzschild found this first exact solution of Einstein’s equations in 1915, while serving as a soldier on the Russian front.

The funnel is the true shape of space around the mass, a surface called Flamm’s paraboloid. Distances along it are real distances. The time part can’t be drawn, but every path here is computed from the full equations, time included. Distances are in units of rs, the horizon’s radius.

Probe. Far out, an orbit closes almost like Newton’s ellipse. Closer in, each lap overshoots, so the ellipse turns and traces a rosette. Mercury does this too, by 43 arcseconds a century, the first evidence that Einstein was right. Inside 3 rs (dashed ice ring) no orbit is stable: give the probe a little less speed and it spirals in through the horizon.

Light. Light passing a mass bends. At 1.5 rs (dashed violet ring) light can orbit, balanced on a knife-edge. Aim any closer than about 2.6 rs and light is captured. That boundary is the black hole’s shadow, photographed by the Event Horizon Telescope for M87* (2019) and for Sagittarius A* at the centre of our galaxy (2022).

Try thisLower the probe’s speed until it dives inside the ice ring. Then switch to light rays and find the aim that loops the hole before escaping. It sits near 2.60 rs.
The math
d²udφ² + u = GML² + 3GMc²u²

The orbit equation in Schwarzschild geometry, with u = 1/r and L the angular momentum per unit mass. Without the last term this is Newton’s equation, whose solutions are closed ellipses. The extra u² term makes orbits precess and allows the plunge. For light, drop the GM/L² term.

Key radii: the horizon at rs; the photon sphere at 1.5 rs; the innermost stable circular orbit at 3 rs; the shadow’s edge at an aim of (√27/2) rs ≈ 2.60 rs. The funnel’s height is z = 2√(rs(r − rs)). The model integrates the equation with a 4th-order Runge–Kutta method.

Observations

Where curved spacetime shows itself

1919 · Eclipse

Starlight bent by the Sun

During a total eclipse, Eddington’s expeditions measured stars near the Sun shifted by about 1.75 arcseconds, twice Newton’s prediction and just what Einstein predicted. Today gravitational lensing turns whole galaxy clusters into natural telescopes and maps dark matter.

2015 · LIGO

Ripples in spacetime

On 14 September 2015, LIGO detected two black holes merging 1.3 billion light-years away. The wave stretched its 4 km arms by less than a thousandth of the width of a proton.

2011 · Gravity Probe B

Earth twists spacetime

Four near-perfect quartz gyroscopes in orbit slowly turned as they were carried through Earth’s curved spacetime. They also confirmed frame-dragging: the spinning Earth drags spacetime around with it.

Every day · GPS

Relativity in your pocket

Satellite clocks are deliberately tuned to tick slightly slow before launch, so that once in orbit they match clocks on the ground. Without general relativity, satellite navigation would drift by kilometres a day.
