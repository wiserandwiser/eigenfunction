neutrino oscillations

PARTICLES & FORCES · the ghost particle — particles that pass through the Earth untouched, and change identity as they fly

Every second, about 65 billion neutrinos from the Sun pass through your thumbnail. Almost none of them touch anything. For thirty years, the few that were caught didn’t add up: only a third of the Sun’s neutrinos seemed to arrive. The answer was that neutrinos shapeshift in flight, which means they must have mass, something the Standard Model said they didn’t.

01Ghost particles

A light-year of lead

Through your thumbnail—
Stopped by the wall—
Stopped by the whole Earth—

Neutrinos are made in vast numbers by nuclear reactions: in the Sun’s core, in reactors, in supernovae and in cosmic-ray showers. They have no electric charge and don’t feel the strong force. They interact only through the weak force, which, as the name says, is very weak.

A typical solar neutrino would sail through about a light-year of solid lead before it had an even chance of being stopped. The whole Earth stops roughly one in a billion. Night-time sunlight is blocked by the planet; night-time solar neutrinos come up through it, and you are bathed in them day and night.

Catching them takes enormous detectors. Super-Kamiokande in Japan is a tank of 50,000 tonnes of ultra-pure water a kilometre underground, lined with 11,000 light sensors, and catches a few dozen solar neutrinos a day.

The math
P(stop) = 1 − e−x/λ,  λ = 1/(nσ) ≈ 1 light-year of lead

For neutrinos of around 1 MeV, the cross-section σ is about 10⁻⁴⁴ cm² per nucleon; the exact length depends steeply on energy. The solar neutrino flux at Earth is about 6.5 × 10¹⁰ per cm² per second. The Earth is treated as about half as dense as lead, averaged over its diameter.

02Shapeshifting

A neutrino that changes flavour in flight

Detector distance—
Chance it has changed flavour—
One full oscillation every—

A neutrino is born in one flavour: electron, muon or tau, set by the particle it was made with. But the flavours are not the states with definite mass. Each flavour is a quantum superposition of three mass states, like a chord made of three notes.

If the masses differ, the three parts travel with slightly different wave rhythms. As the neutrino flies they drift in and out of step, and the mix that makes “electron” turns into the mix that makes “muon” and back again. Catch it at the right distance and it has changed identity.

How far it travels before switching depends on its energy divided by the difference in the squares of the masses. The amplitude depends on the mixing angle. If neutrinos were massless, nothing would ever change: seeing oscillations proves they have mass.

The math
P(να→νβ) = sin²2θ · sin²(1.27 Δm² L/E)

The two-flavour approximation, with Δm² in eV², L in km and E in GeV. T2K: muon neutrinos at about 0.6 GeV over 295 km, Δm² ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻³ eV², near-maximal mixing; most of the missing muon neutrinos have become tau neutrinos, with a few per cent turning into electron neutrinos. KamLAND: reactor antineutrinos at a few MeV, average distance about 180 km, Δm² ≈ 7.5 × 10⁻⁵ eV², sin²2θ ≈ 0.85. Real beams have a spread of energies, which blurs the wiggles at large distances.

03A 30-year mystery

The missing solar neutrinos

Electron-flavour detector sees—
All-flavour detector sees—
Verdict—

In 1968 Ray Davis filled a tank with 600 tonnes of dry-cleaning fluid at the bottom of the Homestake gold mine. Every few weeks he extracted the handful of argon atoms made when a solar electron neutrino hit a chlorine atom. He found only about a third of what John Bahcall’s model of the Sun predicted.

Either the Sun was misunderstood or neutrinos were. For three decades physicists argued. In 1998 Super-Kamiokande found that muon neutrinos from cosmic rays coming up through the Earth were missing compared with those coming down: they were changing flavour over the longer path.

In 2001 the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in Canada settled it with heavy water, which could count electron neutrinos alone and all flavours together. The total matched Bahcall’s prediction exactly; only a third arrived as electron neutrinos. The Sun was fine. The rest had changed on the way (helped by an effect of the Sun’s dense matter, the MSW effect).

The math
SNO: φe / φtotal ≈ 0.34,  φtotal ≈ 5 × 10⁶ cm⁻² s⁻¹ (boron-8 neutrinos)

Measured fractions for the high-energy boron-8 solar neutrinos that SNO could see; Davis’s chlorine experiment found about 2.6 of a predicted 7.6 units. Over the Sun–Earth distance the fast oscillations average out, and inside the Sun the MSW effect converts higher-energy neutrinos more completely, so the surviving fraction depends on energy.

Milestones

1930

Pauli’s “desperate remedy”

Wolfgang Pauli proposed an invisible particle to save energy conservation in radioactive decay, and worried he had predicted something that could never be detected.

1956

First detection

Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines caught antineutrinos from the Savannah River nuclear reactor and telegraphed Pauli the news.

1987

Supernova 1987A

Detectors in Japan and the US caught about two dozen neutrinos from a star that exploded in a neighbouring galaxy (Φ 017).

2002 · 2015

Nobel Prizes

Raymond Davis and Masatoshi Koshiba (2002) for detecting cosmic neutrinos; Takaaki Kajita and Arthur McDonald (2015) for showing that neutrinos oscillate, so have mass.

Note to self

Neutrinos barely interact, yet they change flavour as they fly, which proves they have mass; the Standard Model must be extended to explain it.
