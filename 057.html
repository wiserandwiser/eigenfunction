near-death experiences

FRINGE SCIENCE · NDEs — vivid, structured experiences reported by people who come close to death, and the question of whether consciousness can continue when the brain stops

Around one in ten people revived after cardiac arrest describe something remarkable: deep peace, a light, meeting relatives who have died, watching their own resuscitation from above. The experiences are consistent across cultures and ages, and many people say they are more real than ordinary life. A growing group of physicians, among them cardiologists, neurosurgeons and intensive-care doctors, argue that the medical evidence points beyond the brain. Here you can test the physiological explanations against hospital data, follow a verified out-of-body report minute by minute, and compare NDE memories with real ones.

Does oxygen loss predict the experience?

Most NDE accounts are collected years later. In the 1990s the Dutch cardiologist Pim van Lommel took a different approach: his team interviewed every survivor of cardiac arrest in ten hospitals within days, whether they reported anything or not, and recorded the medical details of each case.

That makes a direct test possible. If NDEs come from oxygen starvation, drugs or fear, then patients with longer arrests, more medication or more fear should report them more often.

The case forPublished in The Lancet in 2001, the study of 344 survivors found that 18% reported an NDE, and none of the medical or psychological factors, including the length of the arrest, the drugs given and fear of death, predicted who had one. A follow-up eight years later found the experiences had lasting effects on people’s lives. Van Lommel argued that if a physiological cause were responsible, most patients who were clinically dead should have had one.
The case againstThe study could not measure brain activity directly, and factors that weren’t recorded, such as the depth of oxygen loss in the brain itself, might still matter.

The AWARE study, led by the resuscitation specialist Sam Parnia, followed 2,060 cardiac arrests in 15 hospitals in the UK, the US and Austria. Survivors were interviewed in depth about any memories from their resuscitation.

One 57-year-old man described watching from the corner of the ceiling as staff worked on him, and gave details that matched the medical record, including two bleeps of an automated defibrillator separated by a pause. Those bleeps fixed the timing: he was describing events about three minutes after his heart had stopped.

The case forThe brain stops producing measurable activity within roughly 20 seconds of the heart stopping, yet this patient was conscious, perceiving and forming memories minutes later, and his account was checked against the record. Parnia’s team, publishing in Resuscitation, noted that this contradicts the idea that NDEs happen only just before arrest or during recovery. In AWARE II (2023), survivors again reported lucid experiences during CPR.
The case againstNo one in either study identified the hidden images placed on high shelves, and hearing, which can persist longest, may explain details such as the bleeps.

03Liège, 2013

Realer than real

Dreams and hallucinations fade quickly and are recalled with less detail than real events. If NDEs are hallucinations of a failing brain, their memories should look the same.

In 2013 Marie Thonnard, Steven Laureys and colleagues at the Coma Science Group in Liège asked NDE survivors to rate three memories each, an NDE, a real event and an imagined event from the same period of life, using a standard Memory Characteristics Questionnaire.

The case forPublished in PLoS ONE, the study found that NDE memories were richer than memories of real events, with more detail, emotion and self-reference, and far richer than imagined ones. People often describe the experience decades later as more real than reality. At the University of Virginia, Bruce Greyson found that people’s accounts stayed consistent when they were re-interviewed about twenty years later, unlike ordinary memories, which drift.
The case againstHighly emotional events of any kind are remembered vividly, so richness may reflect how intense the experience was rather than where it came from.

Note to self

NDEs are unrelated to the usual medical suspects, can be timed to minutes after the heart stops and are remembered as more real than reality; for many researchers, consciousness may not end where the brain does.
