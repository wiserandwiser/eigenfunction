QUANTUM · cryptography — sharing a secret over a line where any eavesdropper is bound to leave fingerprints

Every secret code needs a shared key, and every key has to travel somehow. Send it on single photons and physics itself becomes the alarm: measuring a photon disturbs it, so a spy can’t listen without introducing errors you can count.

The protocol is BB84, invented by Charles Bennett and Gilles Brassard in 1984. Alice sends single photons, each carrying one bit in its polarisation. For each photon she picks, at random, one of two styles: upright (+, where vertical means 0 and horizontal means 1) or diagonal (×, where one diagonal means 0 and the other means 1).

Bob doesn’t know which style she used, so he guesses for each photon. If he guesses right, he reads her bit perfectly. If he guesses wrong, quantum mechanics gives him a coin toss. This is the half-angle rule from Φ 009: a 45° mismatch gives 50/50.

Afterwards they compare styles in public, but never the bits. They keep only the rounds where the styles matched, about half, and throw the rest away. The kept bits are identical at both ends, a shared secret key that never travelled in readable form.

Try thisSend photons one by one and follow the table. Notice the mismatched-style rounds: Bob’s bit there is random, which is why they are discarded.
The math
P(Bob’s bit = Alice’s) = 1 (same style),  ½ (different style)

The two styles are “mutually unbiased bases”: a state that is certain in one gives completely random results in the other, because the angle between them is 45° for photons (90° on the sphere). On average half the rounds survive sifting, and in a perfect channel those have zero errors.

Enter Eve, who taps the line. She can’t copy a photon (the no-cloning rule from Φ 011), so she has to measure it and send on a replacement. But she doesn’t know Alice’s style either. Half the time she guesses wrong, scrambles the photon’s polarisation, and sends Bob the wrong style.

On those photons, even when Bob’s style matches Alice’s, he now gets a random bit. Errors appear in the key: a quarter of every bit Eve touched comes out wrong.

So Alice and Bob sacrifice a small random sample of their key and compare it in public. An unexpected error rate means someone was listening, so they throw the key away and try again. Eve can’t hide by being clever. The disturbance comes from the physics of measurement itself.

Try thisSet Eve to 0% and run: no errors. Then 20%, 60% and 100%, and check that the error rate tracks a quarter of her share every time.

Intercept-and-resend: Eve picks the wrong style with probability ½, and then Bob’s matched-style result is wrong with probability ½, giving ¼ per intercepted photon. On intercepted bits Eve’s own guess is right 75% of the time. More subtle attacks trade information for disturbance, but information-theoretic proofs bound every attack.

03Real links

How much error, and how far?

Real channels have some errors even with no spy, from imperfect optics and detector noise. Alice and Bob can’t tell innocent errors from Eve, so they assume the worst. They fix the errors with public error correction, then shrink the key with privacy amplification, distilling a shorter key that Eve provably knows almost nothing about.

The cost rises with the error rate. Above about 11% nothing secret survives at all. Distance is the other enemy: glass fibre absorbs light, so photons fade exponentially, and single photons can’t be amplified without being measured. That is why records now come from new tricks and from space. China’s Micius satellite set up keys for a 2017 intercontinental video call between Beijing and Vienna, and fibre experiments have reached about 1,000 km.

Meanwhile, ordinary encryption is being upgraded too. In 2024 the US standards body NIST published its first “post-quantum” algorithms, built to resist the quantum computers of Φ 015, which could otherwise decrypt today’s recorded traffic years from now.

r = 1 − 2h(Q),  h(Q) = −Q log₂Q − (1 − Q) log₂(1 − Q)

The Shor–Preskill secure fraction for BB84: one h(Q) pays for error correction and one for privacy amplification. It reaches zero at Q ≈ 11.0%. Fibre loss is about 0.2 dB per km, so survival is 10−0.02L, and about half the survivors are sifted.

Observations

Where quantum security stands

1991 · Ekert

Keys from entanglement

Artur Ekert proposed building keys from entangled pairs, using a Bell test (Φ 010) as the security check. If the pairs still beat the classical limit, nobody can have tampered with them.

Practice

Attacks on the hardware

The maths is sound, but real devices can leak. Researchers have blinded commercial detectors with bright light to steal keys. Newer “device-independent” schemes aim to stay secure even with untrusted equipment.

Now

Harvest now, decrypt later

Adversaries can record encrypted traffic today and wait for a quantum computer. That is why governments are already migrating to post-quantum encryption.

Next

A quantum internet

Combine quantum keys, teleportation (Φ 011) and repeaters and you get networks that link quantum computers and sensors, with security guaranteed by physics.

Note to self

You can’t look at a quantum state without changing it. Turn that weakness into an alarm and you get a key that no eavesdropper can steal unnoticed.
