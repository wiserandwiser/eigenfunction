telepathy

FRINGE SCIENCE · mind-to-mind communication — the claim that thoughts, images or feelings can pass between people without any known sense

The word was coined in 1882 by Frederic Myers of the Society for Psychical Research, and in the 1930s J. B. Rhine brought telepathy into the laboratory at Duke University. Ninety years on, it is one of the most tested claims in psychology, and its supporters point to a stubborn pattern: scores above chance with the sender in another building, results that don’t fade with distance, and pooled evidence that keeps adding up. Here you can rerun the classic distance test, separate sender and receiver, and see how a small effect hides inside many “failed” studies.

01Pearce–Pratt, 1933–34

Hits across a campus

Hits so far—
Expected by chance—
Odds of this by luck—

Rhine’s team used Zener cards: five symbols, five of each in a pack, so blind guessing scores one hit in five. In 1933–34 his assistant J. Gaither Pratt sat in the Social Science Building at Duke and turned the cards one by one at agreed times, while the divinity student Hubert Pearce sat in the library about a hundred metres away and wrote down his guesses.

Both records were sealed and handed to Rhine separately before anyone compared them. Over 1,850 guesses Pearce scored 558 hits where luck predicts 370: about 30% instead of 20%.

The case forWith sender and receiver in different buildings, ordinary sensory cues were ruled out, and the records were sealed before checking. Odds against chance were reported at around 1022 to one. Rhine and Pratt published a full review in 1954, and Pearce’s scores fell only when he was distracted or unwell, a decline that proponents see as the signature of a human ability rather than a trick.
The case againstC. E. M. Hansel argued in 1961 that Pearce was not supervised during the sessions and could, in principle, have seen the cards. No evidence that he did so was ever found.
Try thisLeave the hit rate at Pearce’s 30% and run the series; watch the line leave the chance band within a few hundred guesses. Then lower it to 22% and see how much longer a modest talent takes to show.
The math
z = (hits − Np) / √(Np(1 − p)),  p = 1/5

For Pearce, z = (558 − 370) / √(1,850 × 0.2 × 0.8) ≈ 10.9. The odds are a one-sided probability from the normal approximation to the binomial distribution. Each run here draws guesses at the hit rate you set.

02Separation

Does the effect survive the wall?

Hit rate over 500 trials—
Chance—
Hits owed to ordinary cues—

The first objection to any telepathy result is leakage: a glance, a reflection, a pause, the sound of a pencil. The psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed in 1907 that the horse Clever Hans “counted” by reading his questioner’s posture, so the answer is to separate the people completely.

Parapsychologists did exactly that, moving receivers to other rooms, other buildings and eventually other cities. What proponents find telling is what happened next: in the best-known series the hits did not drop to chance, and they did not weaken with distance either, which is not how any ordinary signal behaves.

The case forRhine’s distance series, the Pearce–Pratt tests and later long-distance studies all report above-chance scoring with every sensory route closed. In 2003 Rupert Sheldrake reported that people guessed which of four familiar callers was phoning about 45% of the time against 25% expected, including in sessions filmed from start to finish. Proponents argue that if cues were the explanation, scores should collapse once cues are impossible; in these studies they did not.
The case againstSceptics reply that across the whole literature the largest effects came from the loosest early studies, and that many tightly controlled replications scored close to chance.
Try thisStart at the same table and move the receiver further and further away. Watch the violet cue share disappear while the pink claimed effect stays the same. Then set the claimed effect to zero to see what sceptics expect instead.
The math
hit rate = ⅕ + ⅘ × (share of trials with a usable cue) + claimed effect

The cue share falls from 12% of trials at a table to 4% behind a screen and zero once the pair are in separate buildings. The claimed effect is set as extra hits per 100 trials beyond chance and, as proponents report, does not depend on distance. Each setting runs 500 simulated trials.

03Pooling the evidence

A small effect hides in small studies

Studies reaching “significant”—
All 200 combined—
Odds of the combined result by luck—

A result is usually called significant if luck alone would produce it less than 5% of the time. If an effect is real but small, a single study of a few hundred trials will often miss it, so a literature full of “failed replications” is exactly what a small, genuine effect predicts.

The way to see such an effect is to fix the method in advance and pool many studies. That is what modern parapsychology does: experiments are pre-registered, their size and analysis set before any data come in, so nobody can stop while ahead.

The case forSince 2012 the Koestler Parapsychology Unit at Edinburgh has run a public registry for parapsychology studies, and pre-registered experiments continue to report small positive effects. In 2018 the psychologist Etzel Cardeña reviewed the field in American Psychologist, the flagship journal of the American Psychological Association, and concluded that the cumulative evidence for psi is comparable to that for many accepted findings in psychology.
The case againstCritics say a field can pool its way to significance through small biases in many studies, and that the largest pre-registered replications in psychology have often found results consistent with chance.
Try thisKeep a small effect of 2.5 extra hits per 100 and count how many single studies “fail”. Then look at the combined result. Raise the trials per study and watch individual studies start to succeed.
The math
combined z = Σzi / √k,  single study significant if zi > 1.645

Each simulated study tests one receiver over a fixed number of trials with chance at one in five plus the effect you set. Studies are combined with Stouffer’s method; the bars show each study’s z-score.

Milestones

1882

A new word

Frederic Myers coined “telepathy” as the Society for Psychical Research was founded in London to investigate such claims scientifically.

1934

Rhine’s ESP

J. B. Rhine published Extra-Sensory Perception, describing card tests at Duke University and popularising the term ESP.

2003

Who’s calling?

Rupert Sheldrake reported that people guessed which of four callers was phoning about 45% of the time, against 25% expected, including in filmed sessions.

2018

A mainstream review

Etzel Cardeña’s review of psi research in American Psychologist argued that the evidence deserves serious attention from mainstream science.

Note to self

Telepathy has scored beyond chance with every sense blocked for ninety years; its supporters say the question is no longer whether there is an effect, but what it is.
