next-gen solar

BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · energy — why a solar cell can never catch all the sunlight, and how stacking two materials breaks the ceiling

Solar panels have become the cheapest electricity ever generated, yet a typical one turns only about a fifth of the sunlight hitting it into power. Some of that loss is unavoidable physics: a single material can never pass about one third. A new crystal, the perovskite, layered on top of silicon is now beating that ceiling in the lab.

01The band gap

Where the sunlight goes

Passes straight through—
Lost as heat—
Left for electricity (at most)—

In a semiconductor, electrons can only absorb light that gives them enough energy to jump a band gap, from being stuck to an atom to moving freely. Each photon lifts at most one electron.

That creates two unavoidable losses. Photons with less energy than the gap pass straight through, wasted. Photons with more energy do lift an electron, but the excess is shaken off as heat within a trillionth of a second, and the electron is left at the bottom of the band. A blue photon carries nearly twice the energy of a red one, but it makes the same one electron.

A small gap catches most photons but keeps little from each; a large gap keeps more from each but misses many. Silicon’s 1.12 eV gap sits near the sweet spot.

02The ceiling

The Shockley–Queisser limit

Theoretical limit—
Nearest real material—

In 1961 William Shockley and Hans Queisser added one more unavoidable loss. A solar cell is warm, and anything that can absorb light can also emit it. Some freed electrons fall back and give their energy away as light before they reach the wires. Balancing light in against light out sets a hard ceiling.

For a single material under ordinary sunlight, the best possible efficiency is about one third, for a band gap near 1.3 eV. Silicon cells in the lab now reach about 27.8%, very close to their practical limit, after 70 years of refinement.

Perovskites are a family of crystals, often lead-halide salts, that can be printed from solution at low temperature. In 15 years their efficiency rose from 3.8% to about 27%, a pace silicon took decades to match. Their band gap can be tuned by swapping atoms. Their weakness is durability: moisture, heat and light can break them down.

03Breaking the ceiling

Stack two materials

Tandem limit—
vs best single layer—
Currents matched?—

The way around the ceiling is to give each colour its own material. A tandem cell puts a wide-gap layer on top to catch the high-energy blue and green light efficiently. It is transparent to red and infrared, which pass through to a narrow-gap layer below.

The two layers are wired in series, so the same current must flow through both. The gaps must be chosen so each layer makes about the same number of electrons, or the weaker one holds back the other.

Silicon makes a near-ideal bottom layer, and a perovskite tuned to about 1.7 eV makes a near-ideal top. Perovskite-on-silicon cells passed silicon’s practical limit in 2020 and reached about 34.9% in 2025. The first commercial modules are now shipping, but they must prove they can last 25 years on a roof.

With the blackbody Sun used here the two-layer limit is about 42% (about 45% under real sunlight). Infinitely many layers under concentrated sunlight could, in principle, reach 86%.

Observations

Solar milestones

1954

The first silicon cell

Bell Labs demonstrated a 6% efficient silicon solar cell. Four years later, Vanguard 1 became the first solar-powered satellite.

2009

The first perovskite cell

Tsutomu Miyasaka’s group in Japan reported a 3.8% perovskite cell. Interest exploded after 2012, when solid-state versions passed 10%.

Space

Multi-junction cells

Satellites and Mars rovers use stacks of three or more III–V semiconductor layers, passing 30% in space. Under concentrated light, six-junction cells have exceeded 47%.

Price

The learning curve

Solar module prices have fallen by more than 99% since 1976, roughly 20% for every doubling of production. In much of the world it is now the cheapest new electricity.

Note to self

One material can turn at most about a third of sunlight into electricity; stacking a perovskite on silicon splits the light by colour and breaks that ceiling.
