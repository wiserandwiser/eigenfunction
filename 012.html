decoherence

QUANTUM · decoherence — a superposition leaking into its surroundings until its quantum behaviour can no longer be seen

If everything is quantum, why does nothing around you ever seem to be in two places at once? Because nothing around you is ever alone. Every stray air molecule and photon carries away a little information, and that leak is enough to make the quantum world look classical.

01The leak

Let the air in and the stripes fade

Chance of a collision—
Stripe contrast—
Molecules landed0

Go back to the double slit of Φ 007, firing large molecules this time. In a perfect vacuum they make clean interference stripes.

Each molecule is drawn as two faint dots, one through each slit, because until something records its path it really does take both. Now let a little gas into the chamber. Each time a molecule bumps a gas particle, the gas particle bounces off in a slightly different direction depending on which slit the molecule was passing. Nobody needs to read that record. The fact that it now exists in the environment is enough, just like the which-path detector in Φ 007.

The stripes fade, and the more gas, the faster. In 2003 a Vienna group did exactly this with C₇₀ molecules and measured the fading, in step with the collision rate. A year later they did it with heat instead: molecules hot enough to glow gave off photons that carried away the path information.

That is decoherence. Large objects are constantly hit by air, light and heat, so their superpositions leak away almost instantly. That is why you never see a chair in two places.

Try thisStart in vacuum and let the stripes form. Then add gas and clear the screen at each step. Watch the contrast drop as the violet collision flashes become common.
The math
V = V₀ e−nσL

The fringe visibility falls exponentially with gas density n, collision cross-section σ and path length L: a molecule that scatters anything loses its interference, while one that slips through untouched keeps it. Formally, the environment becomes entangled with the path, |L⟩|eL⟩ + |R⟩|eR⟩, and interference survives only in proportion to the overlap ⟨eL|eR⟩.

02Losing the phase

A superposition that drifts out of step

Time—
Coherence left—
What it means—

A qubit in superposition is an arrow on the equator of its sphere. Its direction, the phase, holds the quantum information. Here are 200 copies, all prepared identically.

Each copy sits in a slightly different, slightly noisy environment, so each one’s phase drifts at its own rate. The copies fan out. Any single copy is still a perfectly good superposition, but the average, which is what any experiment actually measures, shrinks towards nothing. The quantum coherence has gone.

Part of the fan-out is static: each copy just runs slightly fast or slow. That part can be undone. Erwin Hahn found in 1950 that flipping every arrow halfway through makes the fast ones fall behind by exactly as much as they had gained, so they all come back together. This spin echo is used in every MRI scanner. What it can’t undo is the truly random part, and that sets the qubit’s real lifetime.

Try thisWatch the coherence collapse. Then tick Spin echo and prepare again: the arrows refocus at the end, but never quite all the way. Raise the noise to see the irreversible part grow.
The math
|ψ⟩ = (|0⟩ + eiφ|1⟩)/√2,  |⟨eiφ⟩| → e−t/T₂

The off-diagonal element of the density matrix, the coherence, decays as phases spread. Each copy here gets a fixed random frequency offset (inhomogeneous, reversible) plus a random walk (homogeneous, irreversible). The echo reverses phases at the halfway time τ, cancelling the fixed part at 2τ.

03Engineering

The race to keep qubits coherent

Coherence time—
Operations before it fades—
Milestone—

A quantum computer is a machine for holding superpositions long enough to calculate with them. Decoherence is its main enemy.

The first superconducting qubit (1999) stayed coherent for about a nanosecond, barely long enough for anything. Better circuit designs, purer materials and 3D cavities pushed that up roughly a million-fold, to around a millisecond in the best devices today.

What matters is how many operations fit inside that window. Divide the coherence time by the time per operation and you get a rough budget of steps before the quantum information blurs away. Even that isn’t enough for the biggest algorithms, which is why error correction, in Φ 015, matters so much.

A note of honesty: decoherence explains why superpositions become impossible to observe. It doesn’t by itself explain why one particular outcome happens. That question, the measurement problem, is still debated.

Observations

Decoherence everywhere

Theory

Quantum Darwinism

Wojciech Zurek’s idea: the environment keeps many redundant copies of only certain properties, like position. Those “fittest” properties are the ones every observer agrees on, which may be why the world looks objectively classical.

Biology

Quantum effects in warm, wet life?

Coherence is fragile in warm, noisy cells. Researchers still debate whether quantum effects help photosynthesis or birds’ magnetic compasses, and decoherence times are at the centre of that debate.

Medicine

Echoes in MRI

Spin echoes, and the rates at which signals fade, are how MRI scanners tell tissues apart. Different tissues dephase at different speeds, and that difference becomes contrast in the image.

Shielding

Colder, darker, quieter

Quantum computers sit in dilution refrigerators at around 10 millikelvin, inside metal shields, to starve their qubits of the heat and stray radiation that cause decoherence.

Note to self

Superpositions don’t vanish. They leak into the environment, and once the world holds a record of which way things went, the quantum overlap is gone from sight.
