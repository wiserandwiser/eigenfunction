the three-body problem

ASTROPHYSICS · celestial mechanics, chaos — two bodies dance forever; add a third and the future becomes impossible to predict

Newton solved the motion of two bodies under gravity exactly: neat ellipses, repeating forever. Add a third and there is, in general, no formula at all. The motion can be orderly for millions of years, then suddenly fling one body out of the system. It was here, in 1889, that Henri Poincaré first glimpsed chaos.

01Add a third

A pair of stars, and a third that comes to visit

Stability rule says—
What happened—
Orbits completed—

Two stars in orbit trace the same ellipses forever. That is Newton’s great result, and it is why planets return on schedule.

Give the pair a light, distant companion and not much changes: the companion circles the pair as if it were one heavy star. This hierarchical layout is how real triple stars survive, like Alpha Centauri, where Proxima circles the inner pair about once every 550,000 years.

Make the third star heavier, or bring it closer, and its pull tugs the pair apart and back. The three trade energy in close passes, the paths stop repeating, and sooner or later one star, usually the lightest, gets thrown out of the system for good, leaving a tighter pair behind.

02The butterfly effect

Two universes a hair apart

Gap between the two—
Time—
Copies still agree?—

This is the Pythagorean problem, posed by Carl Burrau in 1913: three stars of mass 3, 4 and 5 start at rest at the corners of a 3-4-5 triangle. They fall together, swing through a series of near-collisions, and finally one pair escapes one way and the lightest star the other.

Run it twice, with one star moved by a billionth of the triangle’s size in the second copy. For a while the two are indistinguishable. Then the tiny difference grows, roughly multiplying by the same factor in each time interval, until the two universes have nothing in common.

That is chaos: completely determined by Newton’s laws, yet unpredictable in practice, because no measurement is perfect. Even our own solar system is chaotic: its planets’ positions can’t be predicted beyond about 100 million years.

The math
|δ(t)| ≈ |δ₀| eλt,  predictability horizon ≈ (1/λ) ln(tolerance/|δ₀|)

λ is the Lyapunov exponent. Because the horizon grows only with the logarithm of your precision, ten times better measurements buy a fixed, small extra time. Units: G = 1; Burrau’s masses and triangle. The gap is the distance between matching stars, summed. Integrated with a fourth-order symplectic method and adaptive steps; the exact ending is itself sensitive to numerical precision, which is the point.

03Islands of order

Rare, perfect dances

Found by—
Survives a nudge?—

Amid the chaos there are special, exactly repeating solutions. Euler (1767) found three stars on a rotating line, and Lagrange (1772) three at the corners of a spinning equilateral triangle. In 1993 Cris Moore found, by computer, three equal stars chasing each other around a figure of eight, proved in 2000 by Alain Chenciner and Richard Montgomery.

Since 2013, computer searches have turned up thousands more: butterflies, moths and yin-yangs. But most are unstable: the slightest disturbance and they break apart. The figure of eight is one of the rare ones that shrugs a nudge off.

Lagrange’s triangle survives in nature when one body is much lighter, as with the Trojan asteroids, which share Jupiter’s orbit 60° ahead and behind it.

Observations

Three bodies in the wild

1889

Poincaré’s prize

King Oscar II of Sweden offered a prize for proving the solar system stable. Poincaré won, then found a serious error in his entry. Fixing it, he discovered the tangled behaviour we now call chaos.

4.2 light-years

Alpha Centauri

The nearest star system is a stable hierarchical triple: two Sun-like stars orbiting every 80 years, and Proxima Centauri far out. It inspired Liu Cixin’s novel The Three-Body Problem.

Our galaxy

Hypervelocity stars

When a binary star passes too close to the Milky Way’s central black hole, one star can be captured and the other flung out at over 1,000 km/s, fast enough to leave the galaxy.

1.5 million km

Lagrange points

Spots where a small body can keep pace with two big ones. The James Webb Space Telescope orbits Sun–Earth L2, and Jupiter’s Trojans cluster at its L4 and L5.

Note to self

Two bodies repeat forever; three are, in general, chaotic: perfectly lawful, yet unpredictable, with only rare islands of order.
