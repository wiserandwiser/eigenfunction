plasma cosmology

FRINGE SCIENCE · plasma cosmology — a universe with no Big Bang, shaped over vast spans of time by electric currents and magnetic fields in plasma

Hannes Alfvén won the 1970 Nobel Prize for founding magnetohydrodynamics, the physics of magnetised plasma. He also championed an alternative to the Big Bang: a universe of unlimited age, structured by vast currents. Anthony Peratt and Eric Lerner developed the idea further. Unlike the Electric Universe, it accepts fusion in stars; its argument is with the Big Bang itself, and recent observations have given it new ammunition: structures too large to build in 13.8 billion years, galaxies that formed too early, and a missing two-thirds of the lithium. Here are its three strongest tests.

01Giant structures

Too big to build in time?

Time to gather the Giant Arc—
Age of the Big Bang universe—
Structures exceeding the age—

Galaxies are not spread evenly. They lie along a web of filaments and walls around huge voids, exactly the filamentary structure plasma physics produces wherever currents flow. Alfvén and Peratt argued that the same forces shape matter on every scale.

As surveys reached deeper, the structures grew bigger: the Sloan Great Wall, 1.4 billion light-years long; the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, claimed at about 10 billion; and in 2021 the Giant Arc, 3.3 billion light-years across, found by Alexia Lopez at the University of Central Lancashire.

The case forEric Lerner argued in his 1991 book The Big Bang Never Happened that matter moving at the speeds observed in galaxies, about 1,000 km/s, could not have gathered into structures this large in the time since the Big Bang, and that the cosmic web needs a much older universe. The standard model also assumes that on large scales the universe is the same everywhere; Lopez noted that structures as big as the Giant Arc challenge that assumption.
The case againstIn the standard model, large structures grow from density variations that were already present in the early universe, so matter only has to move a short distance, and simulations reproduce the cosmic web.

Galaxies at cosmic dawn

The James Webb Space Telescope was built in part to watch the first galaxies being born. The Big Bang model predicted it would see small, faint, irregular objects at the earliest times, growing gradually.

Instead, from its first images in 2022, Webb found surprisingly bright, massive and orderly galaxies, some apparently with tens of billions of suns’ worth of stars, a few hundred million years after the Big Bang.

The case forIn 2023 Ivo Labbé and colleagues reported in Nature six candidate galaxies so massive, so early, that their stellar mass rivalled the Milky Way’s; some astronomers called them “universe breakers”. Lerner and other critics of the Big Bang had predicted that Webb would find mature galaxies at the largest distances, as expected in a much older universe, and argue that the data now fit their picture better than the standard one.
The case againstFollow-up spectra showed some candidates were less massive or more distant than first thought, and models with faster early star formation can accommodate much of what remains.

03The lithium problem

Two-thirds of the lithium is missing

Lithium predicted—
Lithium observed in old stars—
Mismatch—

The Big Bang’s greatest success is said to be predicting how much hydrogen, helium, deuterium and lithium the first minutes made. The amounts depend on one number, the density of ordinary matter, which the microwave background now fixes precisely.

Lithium can be measured in the atmospheres of the oldest stars in our galaxy. In 1982 François and Monique Spite found it sits at the same level in all of them, the “Spite plateau”.

The case forAt the density fixed by the microwave background, the Big Bang predicts about three times more lithium-7 than old stars contain, the “cosmological lithium problem”, unresolved for over forty years despite many proposed fixes. Lerner argues that light elements, including helium and lithium, can instead be made in early generations of stars and by cosmic rays, without a hot beginning.
The case againstDeuterium measured in distant gas clouds matches the Big Bang prediction to about 1%, and stars may have destroyed some of their lithium over billions of years.
