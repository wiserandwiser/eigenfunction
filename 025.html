dark energy

ASTROPHYSICS · cosmology — the expansion of the universe is speeding up, and nobody knows why

Since the 1920s we have known the universe is expanding. Gravity should be slowing that expansion down. In 1998 two teams measuring exploding stars found the opposite: for the last several billion years it has been speeding up. Whatever drives it, called dark energy, makes up about 70% of everything.

01Expanding space

Every galaxy sees the others rushing away

Marked galaxy is—
Moving away at—
Speed ÷ distance—

In 1929 Edwin Hubble, building on Georges Lemaître’s 1927 work and Vesto Slipher’s measurements, showed that distant galaxies are receding from us, and the farther they are, the faster they go. Double the distance, double the speed.

That doesn’t put us at the centre. If space itself is stretching evenly, like dots on an inflating balloon or raisins in rising dough, every galaxy sees the same pattern. There is no centre and no edge; the distances between everything simply grow.

The ratio of speed to distance is the Hubble constant, about 70 km/s for every megaparsec (3.26 million light-years). Run the film backwards and everything was together about 14 billion years ago: the Big Bang.

The math
v = H₀ d,  H₀ ≈ 67–73 km/s/Mpc,  1/H₀ ≈ 14 billion years

Two precise methods disagree: the cosmic microwave background implies about 67, while nearby supernovae calibrated with Cepheid stars give about 73. This “Hubble tension” is one of cosmology’s live puzzles. The picture uses 70, a grid spacing of about 100 Mpc, and a hugely sped-up expansion.

02The 1998 surprise

Distant supernovae are too faint

Universe—
Fit to the supernovae—
Expansion today is—

A type Ia supernova is a white dwarf that explodes after growing past its limit (Φ 017). They all peak at almost the same brightness, so how faint one looks tells you how far away it is. Its redshift tells you how much the universe has stretched since the light left.

In the 1990s two rival teams, led by Saul Perlmutter and by Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess, expected to measure how much gravity was slowing the expansion. Instead, distant supernovae looked about 25% fainter than a slowing universe allows. They were farther away than they should be, so the expansion must have sped up while their light was travelling. Both teams shared the 2011 Nobel Prize.

A universe made only of matter (no dark energy) predicts the points should sit below the line of an empty, coasting universe. They sit above it.

03How it ends

The fate of the universe

Age today—
Fate—

Matter’s gravity pulls the expansion back. Dark energy pushes it on. Early on matter dominated, and the expansion slowed; about 5 billion years ago dark energy took over, because as space grows matter thins out while dark energy, if it is a property of space itself, does not.

With enough matter and no dark energy the universe would one day stop and fall back in a Big Crunch. With today’s mix it expands forever, faster and faster: distant galaxies will eventually slip out of sight, leaving our merged home galaxy alone in the dark.

The simplest explanation, Einstein’s cosmological constant, is the energy of empty space (w = −1). But calculations of that energy from quantum physics overshoot the measured value by up to 120 orders of magnitude. If dark energy grows stronger (w below −1) it ends in a Big Rip that tears apart galaxies, then atoms. In 2024–25, the DESI survey found hints that dark energy may be weakening over time, which would rewrite the story again.

Constant w only; DESI’s hint concerns a w that changes with time. Measured today: Ωm ≈ 0.31, ΩDE ≈ 0.69, w ≈ −1 within about 10%.

Observations

Milestones

1917

Einstein’s constant

Einstein added a cosmological constant to keep his universe static. When expansion was found he dropped it; it returned in 1998 as the leading explanation for dark energy.

1927–29

Lemaître and Hubble

Lemaître derived the expansion from Einstein’s equations and estimated its rate; Hubble’s data made it famous. The law is now often called the Hubble–Lemaître law.

1998

The accelerating universe

The High-Z Supernova Search Team and the Supernova Cosmology Project independently found distant supernovae too faint. Nobel Prize, 2011.

2024–25

DESI

A survey mapping the 3D positions of tens of millions of galaxies. Combined with supernovae and the microwave background, its data favour an evolving dark energy at around 3–4 standard deviations, not yet decisive.
