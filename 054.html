psychokinesis

FRINGE SCIENCE · mind over matter — the claim that intention alone can nudge physical events, from falling dice to electronic noise

In 1934 a gambler told J. B. Rhine he could influence dice by willing them. Rhine took the claim into the laboratory, and the question has since moved from dice to random event generators driven by quantum noise. For nearly three decades Princeton’s engineering school hosted a laboratory devoted to it. The effects reported are tiny, a few extra hits in ten thousand, but they have appeared across hundreds of experiments and dozens of labs. Here you can control for loaded dice, watch intention bend a random walk, and ask how many hidden studies it would take to explain the result away.

01Rhine’s dice, 1934–

Willing the dice, with the bias removed

Rhine’s volunteers threw dice, by hand and later by machine, while concentrating on a chosen face. Early results came out above chance, and critics raised the obvious problem: real dice are slightly imperfect. On cheap dice the six face, with the most pips drilled out, is a little lighter and lands face up more often.

The fix is to rotate the target so that every face is aimed for equally often. Any physical bias then helps on some throws and hurts on others, and cancels out. What survives rotation cannot come from the dice themselves.

The case forIn 1991 Dean Radin and Diane Ferrari pooled 148 dice experiments by 52 investigators, from 1935 to 1987, covering about 2.6 million throws, in the Journal of Scientific Exploration. They found an overall effect far beyond chance, and it remained significant in the studies that balanced the target faces, which removes the die-bias explanation. Rhine’s team also found that hits declined down each record sheet, a fatigue pattern typical of human performance, not of loaded dice.
The case againstThe balanced, better-controlled studies showed much smaller effects than the early ones, and critics argue that smaller effects under tighter control is a warning sign.
Try thisSet the effect to zero and aim for six: imperfect dice alone fake an effect. Now rotate the target, and the fake disappears. Finally restore the claimed effect and see what is left once bias can no longer help.
The math
P(face k) = ⅙ + bias × (k − 3.5) / 2.5,  target hit = P(target) + effect

Each run throws 60,000 simulated dice. The claimed effect adds extra target hits per 1,000 throws, whichever face is the target. With rotation, the bias terms average to zero across the six faces.

02PEAR, 1979–2007

Three intentions, one machine

High minus low, in standard errors—
Baseline runs—
Odds of the separation by luck—

A random event generator turns electronic or quantum noise into a stream of random bits, like millions of coin tosses. In 1979 Robert Jahn, dean of engineering at Princeton, founded the Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research lab, PEAR, with the psychologist Brenda Dunne. Volunteers sat at the machine and, in alternating runs, tried to push the count of 1s up, push it down, or leave it alone.

The design is its own control: the same people, the same machine and the same day, with only the intention changing.

The case forOver twelve years and 91 operators, PEAR reported that the high and low runs drifted apart in the intended directions while the baseline runs behaved like chance, with odds against chance of more than ten thousand to one. Each operator showed a consistent personal “signature”. In 1989 Dean Radin and Roger Nelson pooled nearly 600 such experiments from many labs in Foundations of Physics and found the same small effect, while the control runs stayed at chance.
The case againstA large three-lab replication run by PEAR with German partners in 2000 did not reproduce the main effect, and much of PEAR’s total came from a few operators.
Try thisKeep the effect at 2 per 10,000 and slide the trials up and down. With few trials the three traces tangle; with millions, high and low separate while the baseline stays in the chance band.
The math
drift = effect × N,  noise ≈ ½√N,  separation z = (Dhigh − Dlow) / √(2 × N/4)

The effect is the extra probability of a 1 per bit when intending high, and the same deficit when intending low. Drift grows in proportion to the trials, noise only with their square root, so a real effect always emerges in the end.

03The literature

How big is the drawer?

Null studies needed to cancel it—

Hundreds of random event generator studies have been published. In 2006 Holger Bösch, Fiona Steinkamp and Emil Boller combined 380 of them in Psychological Bulletin, one of psychology’s most selective journals. The overall effect was tiny but statistically significant.

The usual worry with a pooled result is the “file drawer”: unexciting studies left unpublished. The question is how many would be needed. If the answer is a handful, the result is fragile; if it is thousands, it is hard to explain away.

The case forReplying in the same journal, Dean Radin, Roger Nelson, York Dobyns and Joop Houtkooper argued that cancelling the result would take thousands of missing studies, far more than a small field could have run and hidden. They pointed out that the control runs in these experiments sit at chance, so the equipment itself is not biased, and that the effect appears across many independent laboratories.
The case againstBösch and colleagues noted that the smallest studies show the largest effects, a pattern that publication bias can produce, and the effect is close to zero in the very largest studies.
Try thisAt 380 studies, read off how many null studies would be needed to cancel the combined result. Lower the true effect to zero and watch the experimental studies line up with the control runs.
The math
combined Z = Σzi / √k,  fail-safe N = (Σzi)² / 2.706 − k

Rosenthal’s fail-safe number is how many unpublished studies averaging exactly chance would pull the combined result back to the edge of significance. The true-effect slider sets how far above zero each simulated study’s expected z-score sits; the control runs share the same random noise with no effect.
