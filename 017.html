how stars die

ASTROPHYSICS · stellar evolution — a star’s whole life, written in one number: the mass it is born with

A star is a long fight between gravity pulling in and fusion pushing out. Fusion always runs out in the end. What happens next, whether a quiet fade, a glowing cloud or a blast seen across galaxies, depends almost entirely on how heavy the star was at birth.

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram plots stars by brightness (up) against surface temperature (hot on the left, by tradition). Most stars sit on one diagonal band, the main sequence, where they spend about 90% of their lives quietly fusing hydrogen into helium.

Heavier stars sit higher and further left, hotter and far brighter, and they burn out far faster. The Sun gets about 10 billion years. A star 20 times heavier is thousands of times brighter and lasts only about 8 million.

When the core’s hydrogen runs out, the core shrinks and heats while the outer layers swell and cool. The star leaves the band and climbs up and to the right, becoming a red giant. In about 5 billion years the Sun will do this, swallowing Mercury and Venus. Its outer layers will then drift away as a glowing planetary nebula, leaving a hot, tiny core that slides down the diagram as a fading white dwarf.

02The last days

A heavy star becomes an onion, then implodes

Now fusing—
This stage lasts—
Core temperature—

A star more than about 8 times the Sun’s mass doesn’t stop at helium. Each time a fuel runs out, the core contracts, heats up and ignites the ash of the previous stage. Hydrogen makes helium, helium makes carbon and oxygen, then come neon, oxygen and silicon burning.

The core ends up layered like an onion, each shell burning a different fuel. Each stage is hotter and burns far faster than the last: millions of years for hydrogen, about a year for oxygen, about a day for silicon.

Silicon burning makes iron, and iron is the end of the line. Fusing iron absorbs energy instead of releasing it (see Φ 018). With no fire left to hold it up, the iron core, about the mass of the Sun, collapses in under a second, to a ball about 20 km across. The infall rebounds, and a flood of neutrinos helps drive a shockwave that blows the star apart: a core-collapse supernova. When Supernova 1987A went off, detectors on Earth caught about two dozen of its neutrinos.

Approximate stage lengths and core temperatures for a star of about 25 solar masses, from standard stellar models: hydrogen ~7 million years (40 MK), helium ~700,000 years (200 MK), carbon ~600 years, neon ~1 year, oxygen ~6 months, silicon ~1 day (~3 billion K). The collapse releases about 10⁴⁶ J, about 99% of it as neutrinos.

03What’s left

Birth mass decides the corpse

The smallest stars, red dwarfs, burn so slowly they will shine for trillions of years. None has ever died.

Stars up to about 8 solar masses end as white dwarfs: Earth-sized balls of carbon and oxygen held up by electron degeneracy pressure (Φ 014). A teaspoon weighs several tonnes.

Heavier stars collapse past that limit. Electrons are crushed into protons, making a neutron star about 20 km across with more mass than the Sun, held up by neutron degeneracy. A teaspoon weighs about a billion tonnes. The Crab Nebula is the wreck of a supernova Chinese astronomers recorded in 1054, and at its heart a neutron star spins 30 times a second.

Above roughly 20–25 solar masses, even neutrons give way, and nothing known can stop the collapse: a black hole. The exact dividing lines are still debated, because they depend on spin, chemistry and how much mass a star sheds before it dies.

Observations

Stars to watch

Orion’s shoulder

Betelgeuse

A red supergiant about 550 light-years away, nearing the end. It dimmed dramatically in 2019–2020, most likely from a cloud of dust it ejected. It will go supernova, but probably not for tens of thousands of years.

1054

The Crab Nebula

Chinese astronomers recorded a “guest star” bright enough to see in daylight for weeks. Today its debris is expanding at about 1,500 km/s, powered by the pulsar at its heart.

1987

Supernova 1987A

The nearest supernova seen since the invention of the telescope, in the Large Magellanic Cloud. Its neutrino burst confirmed the theory of core collapse, and in 2024 JWST found signs of the neutron star it left.

Our future

The Sun’s ending

In about 5 billion years the Sun will swell into a red giant, then shed its outer layers into a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf about the size of Earth that cools for trillions of years.

Note to self

A star’s birth mass writes its whole biography: how bright, how long, and whether it ends as a white dwarf, a neutron star or a black hole.
