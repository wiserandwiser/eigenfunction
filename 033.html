metamaterials

BREAKTHROUGH TECHNOLOGY · optics and materials — engineered structures that bend light the wrong way, focus it with a flat slab, and steer it around objects

Ordinary materials get their optical properties from their atoms. Metamaterials get theirs from structure: tiny rings, wires and pillars, arranged in patterns smaller than the wavelength they control. Shape them right and you get behaviour no natural material has, including a negative refractive index and, in principle, invisibility.

01The wrong way

Negative refraction and the flat lens

Ray inside bends—
Angle inside—

When light passes from air into glass it slows down and bends towards the perpendicular, the “normal”. The refractive index n says how much. Every natural transparent material has a positive index.

In 1967 Victor Veselago asked what would happen if a material had both its electric response (ε) and its magnetic response (μ) negative. His answer: a negative refractive index. Light would bend the “wrong” way, staying on the same side of the normal, and the waves’ crests would travel backwards even as energy flowed forwards.

In 2000 John Pendry showed that a flat slab with n = −1 would act as a lens, refocusing light from a point to a point with no curved surfaces at all. In principle it could even recover details finer than a wavelength, beating the diffraction limit (Φ 031).

Try thisSlide the index from glass (1.5) through zero to −1 and watch the ray flip sides. Then switch to Flat lens.
The math
n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂,  n = −√(εμ) when ε < 0 and μ < 0

Snell’s law holds unchanged; a negative n₂ simply gives a negative refraction angle. For the flat lens with n = −1, light from a source a distance s above a slab of thickness d focuses at depth s inside the slab and again at d − s below it. Real negative-index materials absorb strongly, which limits the sharpness.

02Building blocks

Rings and wires: making ε and μ negative

ε (electric)—
μ (magnetic)—
Wave in the material—

No natural material has both ε and μ negative at the same frequency. So in 2000 David Smith, Sheldon Schultz and colleagues built one from two kinds of part, each much smaller than the wavelength.

Thin wires act like a dilute metal: below a cut-off frequency the electrons slosh against the wave, making ε negative. Small split rings are tiny circuits with a gap; near their resonance the current they carry pushes back against the wave’s magnetic field, making μ negative. Where both are negative, waves pass through with a negative index. Where only one is, the material is opaque.

The first demonstrations worked at microwave frequencies, with parts millimetres across. For visible light the parts must be tens of nanometres, and metals absorb heavily there, so optical negative-index materials remain a struggle.

The math
ε(ω) = 1 − ωp²/ω²,  μ(ω) = 1 − Fω²/(ω² − ω₀² + iγω)

The standard textbook models for a wire array (plasma frequency ωp) and a split-ring array (resonance ω₀, filling factor F, small loss γ), here with ωp = 1.2, ω₀ = 0.8 and F = 0.5 in units of a reference frequency. Real parts are plotted; the losses make the material dimmer near the resonance.

03Invisibility

Steering light around an object

Rays blocked—
An observer behind sees—

In 2006 Pendry, Schurig and Smith worked out how to hide an object. Imagine space as a rubber sheet. Stretch a point in it open into a hole, squeezing everything that was there into a ring around it. Light that would have crossed the point now flows around the hole, then carries on exactly as before.

Maxwell’s equations have a remarkable property: stretching space is equivalent to changing materials. So a shell whose ε and μ vary in just the right way through its thickness makes light follow those stretched paths. Anything inside the hole is invisible, and so is the cloak itself. The method, transformation optics, is now used to design all kinds of lenses and devices.

That same year the Duke team built a working cloak, for microwaves, in two dimensions, at one frequency. Visible-light cloaks of anything human-sized remain far off: the needed properties are extreme and work only over a narrow band of colours.

Try thisToggle the cloak on and off. Then make the cloak thinner and watch the rays bend more sharply to get around.
The math
r′ = a + r(b − a)/b,  θ′ = θ  (for r < b)

The rays here are exact for an ideal cloak: each straight path in the undistorted space is carried through this mapping, which squeezes a disk of radius b into the ring between a and b. The material that achieves this is anisotropic, and at the inner edge some of its components must go to zero or infinity, one reason perfect cloaks are impractical.

Observations

Metamaterials in the world

Phones

Metalenses

Flat lenses made of nanoscale pillars, a cousin of metamaterials called metasurfaces. Since 2022 they have shipped in smartphone depth sensors, replacing stacks of curved glass.

Radio

Steerable antennas

Metasurface antennas steer radio beams electronically with no moving parts, used in satellite internet terminals and research on 6G networks.

Sound

Acoustic metamaterials

The same ideas work for sound: structures that block particular frequencies, focus ultrasound, or quieten engines and ventilation without bulky padding.

The ground

Seismic metamaterials

Researchers have tested arrays of boreholes and buried resonators that deflect some surface waves around a protected area, an idea for shielding buildings from earthquakes.

Note to self

Metamaterials get their powers from structure, not atoms: they can bend light the wrong way and, by imitating stretched space, steer it around a hidden object.
