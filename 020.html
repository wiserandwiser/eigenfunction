pulsar timing

ASTROPHYSICS · a galaxy-sized detector — dead stars that tick like atomic clocks, used to feel waves light-years long

Some neutron stars spin hundreds of times a second and sweep a radio beam past Earth on every turn. Their ticks are so regular that astronomers can predict each one years ahead to within a millionth of a second. Watch dozens of them together, and tiny shared wobbles reveal gravitational waves washing through the galaxy.

01The cosmic lighthouse

A city-sized star that spins like a blender

Spins per second—
Speed at its equator—
Shown—

A neutron star (Φ 017) is about 20 km across and keeps the spin of the giant star it came from. Shrinking that much spins it up, as a skater spins faster by pulling in their arms. Its enormous magnetic field funnels radio waves into two beams from its magnetic poles. If the poles are tilted from the spin axis, the beams sweep around like a lighthouse, and if one crosses Earth, we see a pulse on every turn.

In 1967, graduate student Jocelyn Bell noticed a strange “scruff” on her radio chart: pulses every 1.34 seconds, so regular the team half-jokingly labelled it LGM-1, for “little green men”. It was the first pulsar.

The fastest, millisecond pulsars, are old neutron stars spun up by feeding on a companion star. They turn hundreds of times a second, so steadily that a few rival atomic clocks over years.

Speeds use a 12 km radius. The fastest known pulsar, PSR J1748−2446ad, spins 716 times a second, so its equator moves at about a fifth of the speed of light. The animation is slowed so the beams stay visible; the sound plays the real rate. Spins slow very gradually: the Crab’s period grows by about 13 millionths of a second each year.

02Timing a cosmic clock

Peel away everything we know, and look at what’s left

Biggest effect left—
Arrival times wander by—

Astronomers record when each pulse arrives and compare it with a model that predicts every pulse. The difference, the timing residual, is where the science is.

The first thing you see is Earth itself: as we orbit the Sun, we are sometimes closer to the pulsar and sometimes farther, so pulses arrive up to about 8 minutes early or late. Take that out and the pulsar’s own orbit around its companion shows. Take that out and you see the pulsar slowly spinning down. Take that out too, and what is left is scatter of about a ten-millionth of a second, over ten years.

Hidden in that scatter is a slow wander of tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. A gravitational wave light-years long stretches the space between us and the pulsar, so pulses arrive a little early for a few years, then a little late. A single pulsar can’t prove it, though: its own spin may wobble, and our clocks may drift.

03A detector the size of the galaxy

Einstein’s fingerprint across the sky

Pulsar pairs—
Match to the curve—

The trick is to compare pairs of pulsars. A background of gravitational waves, from countless pairs of supermassive black holes across the universe, stretches the space around Earth in a shared way, so every pulsar’s wander has a common part.

General relativity predicts how similar two pulsars’ wanders should be, depending only on the angle between them in our sky: alike when close together, opposite at about 90°, and partly alike again when on opposite sides. This is the Hellings–Downs curve, worked out in 1983. Clock errors or solar-system mistakes would leave different fingerprints.

In June 2023, the NANOGrav collaboration (68 pulsars, 15 years) and teams in Europe, India, Australia and China reported evidence for this curve. The background is most likely from supermassive black-hole pairs, though more exotic sources are not ruled out.

The math
Γ(θ) = ½ − x/4 + (3/2) x ln x,  x = (1 − cos θ)/2

Each pulsar’s data here is simulated as a common wander with exactly these correlations plus independent noise twice as large, so most single pairs are dominated by noise. The number of independent samples grows with the years observed, because the waves are so slow. “Match” is the correlation between the binned averages and the curve. Real analyses must also model each pulsar’s own slow noise, which is why it took over 15 years.

Observations

Pulsars to know

1967 · Vulpecula

PSR B1919+21

The first pulsar, found by Jocelyn Bell Burnell. Its stacked pulses later became a famous album cover. The 1974 Nobel Prize for the discovery went to her supervisor Antony Hewish and to Martin Ryle.

1974

The Hulse–Taylor binary

A pulsar orbiting another neutron star. Its orbit shrinks exactly as gravitational-wave losses predict, the first evidence the waves exist (Φ 019).

2003

The Double Pulsar

PSR J0737−3039: two pulsars orbiting each other every 2.4 hours. It has confirmed general relativity to about 0.01% and shows the time delay of signals passing a heavy star.

1992

The first exoplanets

Planets were first found orbiting a pulsar, PSR B1257+12, through tiny shifts in its pulse times, three years before one was found around a Sun-like star (Φ 023).
