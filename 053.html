remote viewing

FRINGE SCIENCE · seeing at a distance — the claim that a trained person can describe a hidden or distant place they have no ordinary way to know

For more than twenty years, from 1972 to 1995, the United States government paid for remote viewing research, first at Stanford Research Institute and later inside the Army and the Defense Intelligence Agency under names such as Grill Flame and Stargate. Physicists ran the lab work; soldiers trained as viewers. When the programme was reviewed, the statistician brought in to examine the data concluded that the effect was real. Here you can judge a viewing session, try to predict the market with two pictures, and see what a modest talent is worth to a team.

01SRI, 1972–1974

The judge picks the target

In a typical session, an experimenter drives to a site chosen at random from a sealed list, while the viewer, shut in a room miles away, describes and sketches whatever comes to mind. Afterwards an independent judge, who doesn’t know the answer, compares each transcript with the real site and several decoys and ranks them.

Because the judge is blind and the target random, a viewer with no ability should be ranked first only one time in five. The SRI physicists Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff reported far more first-place matches than that, with drawings that sometimes captured a site’s layout in unexpected detail.

The case forTarg and Puthoff published early results in Nature in 1974, a rare step for parapsychology. Their best viewers, including the artist Ingo Swann and the former police commissioner Pat Price, produced sketches such as Price’s drawing of a large gantry crane at a secret Soviet site, details later said to match satellite photographs. Viewers were never told the target, and judges never knew the true answer, the same double-blind logic used in drug trials.
The case againstSceptics note that vague descriptions such as “water… something tall… lines” can fit many places, so the decoy set and the judging protocol matter a great deal.

02Associative viewing, 1982

Nine weeks of silver

Nine out of nine by luck—
Nine out of nine at this hit rate—

Associative remote viewing turns a yes-or-no question about the future into a viewing task. Each possible answer, say “silver rises” or “silver falls”, is paired with a different picture. The viewer describes the picture they will be shown next week; a judge decides which picture the description matches, and that becomes the prediction.

In 1982 Russell Targ and the psychologist Keith Harary used the method to predict the silver futures market for nine weeks in a row.

The case forAll nine predictions were correct, and investors reported earning about $120,000. By chance alone a perfect run of nine binary calls happens once in 512 attempts. Later associative-viewing studies, including a 2014 project by Christopher Carpenter Smith and colleagues on the stock index, have reported above-chance predictions as well.
The case againstAttempts to repeat the silver streak the following year did not succeed, and short runs are easy to celebrate after the fact.
Try thisAt an 80% hit rate, run nine weeks a few times and count the perfect runs. Then set the rate to 50%, a coin toss, and see how rare nine in a row becomes.
The math
P(9 of 9) = p9,  by luck: (½)9 = 1 / 512

Each week is a separate prediction that comes true with the hit rate you set. A viewer who is right 80% of the time completes a perfect nine-week run about once in seven attempts.

031995 · The review

What a modest talent is worth

When the programme was closed, the CIA asked the American Institutes for Research to evaluate it. Two experts reviewed the laboratory data: the statistician Jessica Utts of the University of California, Davis, and the psychologist Ray Hyman.

Utts found that in the best-controlled experiments, run at SAIC between 1989 and 1993, viewers were right about a third of the time where a quarter was expected, a result consistent across viewers and laboratories.

The case forUtts wrote that “using the standards applied to any other area of science, it is concluded that psychic functioning has been well established”. The effect sizes matched across SRI, SAIC and independent labs. A one-third hit rate sounds small, but a team of viewers voting together, as this simulation shows, can turn it into a large advantage, which is why proponents argue the talent had real practical value.
The case againstHyman agreed the results were not due to chance but argued that flaws could not be ruled out without independent replication, and the overall report found the programme had not produced actionable intelligence.

Viewers in uniform

The Army creates a unit of trained remote viewers at Fort Meade, Maryland, including Joseph McMoneagle, later known as Remote Viewer 001.

The Utts report

Reviewing the programme, statistician Jessica Utts concludes that psychic functioning has been well established by the standards of other sciences.

Note to self

Under double-blind judging, remote viewers have been ranked first far more often than chance, and the government’s own statistical reviewer called the effect established.
