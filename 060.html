ganzfeld experiments

FRINGE SCIENCE · the ganzfeld — a telepathy test in which a relaxed receiver, cut off from sight and sound, tries to sense an image a distant sender is concentrating on

A receiver relaxes in a reclining chair, halved ping-pong balls over the eyes, bathed in red light, hearing soft hiss through headphones. In another room, a sender concentrates on a picture or video clip chosen at random by computer. Afterwards the receiver picks the target from four options, so guessing gives 25%. Since the 1970s, lab after lab has reported hit rates around 32%, and the method was designed with a leading sceptic to close every loophole. It is often described as the most replicated experiment in parapsychology. This final lesson shows why its supporters consider the case strong.

01Statistical power

Why real effects “fail” to replicate

Chance the study detects the effect—
Sessions needed for 80%—
Hits needed to be significant—

A hit rate of 32% against 25% chance is a modest effect. Ganzfeld sessions take hours each, so typical studies have only 30 to 50 sessions. How often would a study that small detect such an effect, even if it were perfectly real?

The answer is: less than half the time. A literature of small studies of a real effect will therefore contain many “failures”, and judging the effect by counting successes and failures will badly underrate it.

The case forSupporters, including the statistician Jessica Utts, argue that the mixed record of individual ganzfeld studies is exactly what low power predicts for a real 32% effect, and that it is a statistical error to treat small null studies as refutations. When the studies are pooled, as they should be, the effect is consistent and highly significant.
The case againstLow power also makes a literature vulnerable to selective reporting, and critics say large pre-registered studies should be the decisive test.
Try thisKeep the true rate at 32% and read the chance of detection for 40 sessions. Then slide the sessions up until the curves separate, and read how many a decisive study needs.
The math
power = P(hits ≥ hcrit | p),  hcrit: smallest h with P(≥ h | ¼) ≤ 5%

Exact binomial probabilities. The sessions-needed figure uses the normal approximation for a one-sided test at 5% with 80% power.

02Fifty years of data

The meta-analyses agree

In 1985 the parapsychologist Charles Honorton and the sceptic Ray Hyman analysed the early studies and disagreed. In a rare joint communiqué they agreed on what a better experiment needed: computer randomisation, no handling of target pictures, and documented procedures.

Honorton then built the fully automated “autoganzfeld” to those standards. Its results, published with Daryl Bem in Psychological Bulletin in 1994, came out at 32%, the same as before.

The case forThe sceptic’s own standards did not make the effect disappear. In 2010 Lance Storm, Patrizio Tressoldi and Lorenzo Di Risio’s meta-analysis of 29 newer studies, also in Psychological Bulletin, again found about 32%, and later updates by the same group reported the effect continuing. Even including Milton and Wiseman’s near-chance 1999 set, the combined result stays far above chance.
The case againstCombined results depend on which studies are included and when the analysis cuts off, and Hyman published a reply in the same issue disputing the 2010 conclusion.

Artists, meditators and the rest

Creative arts students—

If telepathy is a human ability, it should vary between people, as every other ability does. Ganzfeld researchers have long reported that some participants score consistently better: people who practise meditation or another mental discipline, those who report previous psychic experiences, and creative people.

In 1992 Marilyn Schlitz and Charles Honorton tested twenty music, drama and dance students at the Juilliard School in New York.

The case forThe Juilliard students scored 50%, double the chance rate, one of the highest results in the literature. Storm and colleagues’ 2010 meta-analysis found that selected participants scored markedly higher than unselected ones, a pattern of individual differences that would be hard to produce by chance or by flaws in the procedure, which were the same for everyone.
The case againstThe high-scoring groups are mostly small, and subgroups found after the fact can look impressive by chance.

Observations

Milestones

1974

The first ganzfeld

Charles Honorton and Sharon Harper, and independently William Braud, began ganzfeld studies, reasoning that quieting the senses might help faint signals.

1986

The joint communiqué

After a public debate, Honorton and the sceptic Ray Hyman published agreed standards for future experiments.

1994

Autoganzfeld

Bem and Honorton reported 32% hits from fully automated studies meeting those standards, in Psychological Bulletin.

2010

Still 32%

Storm, Tressoldi and Di Risio’s meta-analysis of newer studies again reported about 32%, in the same journal.

Note to self

Under protocols agreed with its fiercest critic, the ganzfeld has returned about 32% where chance gives 25% for forty years, its supporters’ strongest evidence that minds can share information.
